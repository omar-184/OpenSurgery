Melanoma

Summary

  • Malignant melanoma is a cutaneous malignancy arising from melanocytes that, although accounting for less than 2% of skin cancer cases, causes the majority of skin-cancer-related deaths and represents about 15% of skin cancers but 65% of skin cancer deaths [1][2].
  • Diagnosis is guided by the ABCDE clinical criteria and confirmed by biopsy, with Breslow thickness the principal determinant of prognosis and of the surgical margin required for wide local excision [1].
  • Management combines wide local excision, sentinel lymph node biopsy for intermediate/thick lesions, and, for high-risk resected or metastatic disease, adjuvant or systemic immunotherapy and BRAF/MEK-targeted therapy [1].

Definition

  • Malignant melanoma is the malignant transformation of melanocytes, which originate from the neural crest and are therefore neuroectodermal in origin; use of the descriptive term "malignant melanoma" (rather than "melanocarcinoma" or "melanosarcoma") is recommended, reserving "mole" or "pigmented naevus" for benign lesions [3].
  • Melanoma originates from neural crest-derived melanocytes in the basal layer of the epidermis [2].
  • A mole becomes a malignant melanoma when melanocytes invade adjacent tissue or show abnormal, excessive multiplication [3].

Pathophysiology

  • Melanoma has the highest mutational burden of any malignancy studied in humans [1].
  • Integrative genomic analysis (The Cancer Genome Atlas) identified four molecular subtypes based on the most prevalent altered genes: mutant BRAF (about half of cutaneous melanomas, most commonly V600E), mutant RAS (about 30%), mutant NF1 (about 15%), and triple-wild-type [1].
  • Nearly all subtypes exert their effect through the MAPK signalling pathway, in which gain-of-function mutations (BRAF, RAS) or loss of inhibitory steps (NF1) cause unchecked cellular growth; the PI3K/AKT pathway is also frequently altered, with loss of the tumour-suppressor PTEN in 25–50% of nonfamilial melanomas contributing to resistance to BRAF/MEK-targeted therapy [1].
  • Additional loss of tumour-suppressor genes is required for full neoplastic development; CDKN2A, which encodes the tumour suppressors INK4A and ARF, is inactivated in many familial melanomas [1].
  • Tumour growth proceeds through an initial radial growth phase confined to the epidermis and superficial dermis, followed by a vertical growth phase in which the lesion becomes raised and palpable and gains access to blood vessels and lymphatics, enabling metastasis [1].
  • Melanoma spreads through intradermal lymphatics to form satellite nodules around the primary lesion, and via lymphatics to regional nodes, with the malignant pigment sometimes spreading diffusely to produce a brown halo in surrounding skin [3].

Clinical features

  • Melanoma commonly presents as an irregular pigmented skin lesion that has grown or changed over time; the ABCDE criteria (Asymmetry, irregular Borders, Color variegation, Diameter greater than 6 mm, Evolution/change over time) guide the decision to biopsy [1][4].
  • Cardinal symptoms of malignant change in a mole are loss of normal surface skin markings, itching (often with a pale pink halo), increase in size/shape/thickness, change in colour (patchy darkening, blue-purple areas from increased vascularity), bleeding (usually slight and a late sign), and satellite nodules or regional lymph node enlargement indicating spread [3].
  • More than 25% of melanomas arise de novo in normal skin rather than in a pre-existing mole [3].
  • The most common site is the back in men and the legs in women; melanoma is rare before puberty and rare in Black individuals compared with White individuals [2][3].
  • Roughly 10% of patients present with regional nodal disease and up to 5% with distant metastases at diagnosis; in-transit metastasis refers to tumour spread within draining lymphatic channels evident as cutaneous/subcutaneous nodules between the primary and regional nodes [1].
  • Amelanotic melanoma presents as a raised pink, purple, or flesh-coloured lesion and is often diagnosed late [4].
  • Blue colouration is the most ominous sign clinically [2].
  • Four major histologic/clinical types exist: superficial spreading melanoma (most common, intermediate malignancy, often on the trunk/proximal extremities, initially flat and radially growing), lentigo maligna melanoma (least aggressive, occurs on sun-exposed skin of older individuals, slow radial growth), acral lentiginous melanoma (most common type in non-White patients, arises on palms, soles, and subungual sites, often diagnosed late with poor prognosis), and nodular melanoma (most aggressive, early vertical growth phase, most likely to have already metastasised at diagnosis, may be amelanotic) [1][2][4].
  • Subungual melanoma is often mistaken for a subungual haematoma; unlike a haematoma, it does not migrate distally with nail growth [1].

Etiology

  • There is a clear association between UV radiation exposure and melanoma development, with intense intermittent sun exposure (severe blistering sunburns) a stronger causative factor than chronic sun exposure; both UVA (implicated in tanning-bed-associated melanoma) and UVB contribute [1].
  • Genetic risk factors include high-risk skin types (Fitzpatrick I and II), family history of melanoma, and xeroderma pigmentosum; patients with a prior melanoma or other skin cancer, or with many melanocytic nevi, dysplastic nevi, or giant congenital nevi, are also at increased risk [1].
  • Dysplastic, atypical, or large congenital nevi confer roughly a 10% lifetime melanoma risk, and familial atypical multiple mole-melanoma ("BK mole") syndrome carries almost 100% risk; about 10% of melanomas are familial, most commonly linked to CDKN2A mutation [1][2].
  • Fair complexion, blonde or red hair, blue eyes, easy sunburning, freckling tendency, and inability to tan are recognised phenotypic risk factors [1][3].
  • Up to 40% of melanomas arise within a pre-existing lesion (dysplastic nevus, congenital nevus, or Spitz nevus) [1].

Diagnosis

  • Skin biopsy techniques include excisional biopsy (complete removal, best for small lesions, oriented to allow subsequent wide excision if needed), incisional/punch biopsy (for larger lesions, minimum 4 mm punch), and shave biopsy (common but risks transecting the lesion and confounding Breslow thickness assessment; deep shave/saucerization to subcutaneous fat mitigates this) [1].
  • Lesions under 2 cm are generally managed with excisional biopsy (unless in a cosmetically sensitive area), while lesions over 2 cm or in cosmetically sensitive areas are managed with incisional or punch biopsy; both require subsequent margin resection if pathology confirms melanoma [2].
  • Ablation of pigmented lesions by cryotherapy, cautery, or laser is discouraged as it delays diagnosis [1].
  • All pigmented lesions are sent for permanent-section pathology; immunohistochemical stains for melanoma markers include S-100, HMB-45, and Melan-1 [1][2].
  • Breslow thickness (vertical thickness from the granular layer to the deepest tumour cell) has largely replaced Clark's level of invasion as the principal prognostic depth measure because of lower interobserver variability; melanomas are classified as thin (<1 mm), intermediate (1–4 mm), or thick (>4 mm) [1].
  • Ulceration (absence of intact epithelium over the tumour histologically) is a critical adverse prognostic factor across all thickness categories [1].
  • Additional workup for localised stage I/II disease is generally unnecessary; imaging (chest/abdomen/pelvis CT, brain MRI) and blood tests (LFTs, LDH) are reserved for thick (T4) primaries, clinically apparent nodal disease, or suspected stage IV disease, and staging LDH/imaging is recommended for melanoma ≥1 mm [1][2].
  • Tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes (classified as brisk, nonbrisk, or absent) indicate host immune response and correlate with more favourable prognosis [1].

Scoring and Severity

  • AJCC 8th edition TNM staging is based on Breslow thickness (T1 ≤1.0 mm, T2 >1.0–2.0 mm, T3 >2.0–4.0 mm, T4 >4.0 mm), with T1 further subclassified by ulceration (T1b = ulceration at any thickness, or thickness 0.8–1.0 mm without ulceration) [1].
  • N stage incorporates the number of involved nodes, nodal tumour burden (clinically occult by SLN biopsy vs clinically apparent disease), and, since the 8th edition, stratifies in-transit/microsatellite/satellite metastases by node number (N1c, N2c, N3c) [1].
  • M stage is stratified by anatomic site (M1a: distant skin/soft tissue/nonregional nodes; M1b: lung; M1c: non-CNS viscera; M1d: CNS) and by serum LDH, with elevated LDH indicating worse prognosis in all M categories [1].
  • Prognosis is worse in older patients, males, and axial (trunk/head/neck) primary sites, and worse for ulcerated, ocular, and mucosal lesions, factors not all captured by the AJCC system [1][2].
  • Clinical staging is based on biopsy and physical examination; pathologic staging additionally requires assessment of regional nodes, usually by sentinel lymph node (SLN) biopsy [1].

Treatment and Management

  • Treatment for all stages combines resection of the primary tumour with appropriate margins (down to the muscle fascia) and management of the regional lymph nodes [2].
  • Recommended wide local excision (WLE) margins by Breslow thickness are: in situ, 0.5 cm; thin (<1 mm), 1 cm; intermediate (1–2 mm), 1–2 cm; thick (>2–4 mm and >4 mm), 2 cm, margins greater than 2 cm have not shown additional benefit in randomized trials (British Collaborative Trial and Swedish Melanoma Study Group), though 1-cm margins are associated with a higher local recurrence risk than 2-cm margins for intermediate-thickness lesions [1][2].
  • Mohs micrographic surgery is not considered oncologically acceptable for melanoma outside highly selected cases at experienced centres (used mainly for melanoma in situ) [1].
  • Adjuvant systemic therapy has transformed outcomes for resected high-risk (stage III) melanoma: dual BRAF/MEK inhibition (dabrafenib/trametinib) for BRAF-mutant disease improved 5-year relapse-free survival from 36% to 52% (COMBI-AD trial); the anti-CTLA-4 antibody ipilimumab improved 5-year overall survival from 54.4% to 65.4% (EORTC 18071) but with substantial toxicity (54% serious adverse events); and PD-1 inhibitors nivolumab and pembrolizumab achieved superior relapse-free survival with a better safety profile (CheckMate 238, EORTC 1325/KEYNOTE-054) [1].
  • Historically, high-dose interferon-alfa-2b was the only approved adjuvant therapy but was poorly tolerated and only marginally effective on disease-free survival with minimal overall survival benefit [1].
  • First-line chemotherapy for metastatic melanoma is dacarbazine; interferon-alpha, immunotherapy, and tumour vaccines are systemic options, and radiotherapy can aid regional control without a proven survival benefit [2].
  • Resection of isolated, low-risk-to-resect metastases (e.g., lung or liver) offers some patients a long disease-free interval and represents the best chance of cure in selected patients with metastatic disease [1][2].

Surgeries

  • Wide local excision is performed to the muscular fascia (fascia excision not routinely required) via a fusiform incision allowing primary closure where possible; frozen section margin analysis is not performed, and rotational flaps or grafts may be needed for larger defects, particularly on the head/neck or distal extremities [1].
  • Subungual melanoma is treated by amputation of the distal digit (distal interphalangeal joint amputation is sufficient for fingers; ray amputation is unnecessary) [1].
  • Sentinel lymph node (SLN) biopsy, introduced by Dr.
  • Donald Morton in 1992, is required for staging melanoma greater than 1.0 mm thick and is performed using combined technetium-99 lymphoscintigraphy and intraoperative vital blue dye injection with a handheld gamma probe; a sentinel node is defined as the most radioactive node, any blue node, any node with ≥10% of the radioactivity of the hottest node, or any palpably abnormal node [1].
  • SLN biopsy should be performed for tumours over 1 mm deep (clinically node-negative), and considered for 0.8–1.0 mm tumours with ulceration, high mitotic index, or lymphovascular invasion [1][2].
  • Elective (upfront) lymph node dissection in clinically node-negative patients is now obsolete, superseded by SLN biopsy [1].
  • Completion lymph node dissection (CLND) after a positive SLN biopsy no longer improves survival per the MSLT-II and DeCOG-SLT randomized trials (equivalent melanoma-specific survival with observation/ultrasound surveillance), so CLND is now reserved for selective circumstances rather than performed routinely [1].
  • Formal (therapeutic) lymphadenectomy is required for clinically positive nodes or a positive SLN biopsy when performed, aiming to clear tumour rather than to stage; axillary dissection removes levels I, II and III (as for isolated axillary nodal melanoma with unknown primary), inguinal dissection covers superficial (and, selectively, deep pelvic) nodes, and cervical dissection is typically a functional neck dissection sparing the internal jugular vein and spinal accessory nerve [1][2].
  • Superficial parotidectomy (or careful parotid SLN removal) is indicated for scalp/face melanomas anterior to the tragus and above the lower lip that are ≥1 mm deep, given a roughly 20% parotid metastasis rate; melanomas posterior to the ear drain to the posterior neck nodes instead [1][2].
  • For head and neck melanoma, margins may be modified where they abut critical structures (e.g., carotid artery) provided tumour-free margins are still achieved, and the facial nerve should be preserved unless already clinically involved [2].

Complications

  • Completion lymph node dissection carries significant short- and long-term morbidity, including wound complications, paraesthesias, and permanent lymphedema, and only 15–20% of patients with a positive SLN have additional nonsentinel node metastases found at CLND, meaning most patients undergoing CLND experience its morbidity without therapeutic benefit [1].
  • Adjuvant immunotherapy carries risk of serious immune-related adverse events; in the EORTC 18071 ipilimumab trial, serious adverse events occurred in 54% of treated patients and 1.1% died of treatment-related complications [1].
  • Wide local excision requiring 2-cm margins in the head/neck or distal extremities may require complex tissue rearrangement or skin grafting for closure [1].

Prognosis

  • Prognosis worsens with increasing Breslow thickness, presence of ulceration, male sex, older age, axial primary site, and ocular or mucosal (versus cutaneous) origin [1][2].
  • In the MSLT-I trial, 10-year melanoma-specific survival for intermediate-thickness melanoma was 85.1% with a negative SLN biopsy versus 62.1% with a positive SLN biopsy (hazard ratio 3.09), confirming SLN status as the strongest predictor of recurrence or death among clinically node-negative patients [1].
  • The lung is the most common site of distant melanoma metastasis, and melanoma is the most common cause of metastasis to the small bowel [2].
  • A prolonged disease-free interval before development of resectable metastasis is associated with a better chance of cure after metastasectomy [2].
