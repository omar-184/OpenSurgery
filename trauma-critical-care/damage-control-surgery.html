Damage Control Surgery

Summary

  • Damage control surgery (DCS) aims to break the "vicious cycle" (or "triad of death") of hypothermia, coagulopathy, and acidosis by performing only the minimum surgery needed to control haemorrhage and contamination, deferring definitive anatomical repair until the patient's physiology has been restored in intensive care [1][2].
  • The concept originated from a naval shipbuilding strategy in which ship damage was kept "local," with only minimal repairs performed to keep the ship afloat until definitive repairs could be made safely in port [2].
  • Rotondo and associates first coined the term "damage control" surgery in a series of 46 patients with penetrating abdominal injury, in which a significant survival improvement was seen in the subset with major vascular injury and two or more visceral injuries (77% vs. 11%, P<0.02) despite similar overall survival between damage control and definitive laparotomy groups (55% vs. 58%) [3].

Definition

  • Damage control surgery is restricted to three goals: stopping active surgical bleeding, controlling contamination, and restoring normal physiology; anatomy is restored only once physiology is optimised [1][2].
  • It is distinguished from "early total care" (ETC), the definitive management of a patient's injuries within 36 hours after a period of initial resuscitation, which is appropriate for the majority of trauma patients who respond well to resuscitation [4].
  • Damage control resuscitation is the broader concept encompassing the resuscitative strategies (permissive hypotension, balanced blood product transfusion, minimal crystalloid) that accompany damage control surgery [2][3].

Pathophysiology

  • A subset of patients undergoing traditional definitive repair at the index operation develop progressive intraoperative physiologic derangement with exacerbation of the lethal triad: hypothermia, coagulopathy, and metabolic acidosis [3].
  • Protracted surgery in a physiologically unstable patient can itself prove fatal; prolonged procedures cause additional trauma and further immune/physiological derangement, propagating the triad of death toward multi-organ failure and death [2][4].
  • Core temperature loss during surgery is typically about 2°C per hour of operating time, which is why operative time itself is a criterion for converting to damage control [2].

Clinical features

Patients requiring damage control typically present with ongoing haemorrhage refractory to initial measures, hypothermia, evidence of coagulopathy (oozing from all surfaces), and severe metabolic acidosis; hemodynamically unstable patients with intra-abdominal bleeding will always proceed to rapid damage control laparotomy, this is often an easy decision for senior clinicians, while other cases require careful review of physiology and coagulation status [4]. Not covered in source textbooks as a distinct further subsection beyond the criteria and physiological indices detailed under Scoring and Severity.

Etiology

Indications for damage control surgery include: inability to achieve haemostasis; complex abdominal injury (e.g., liver and pancreas); combined vascular, solid, and hollow organ injury (e.g., aortic or caval injury); inaccessible major venous injury (e.g., retrohepatic vena cava); the need for non-operative control of other injuries (e.g., fractured pelvis); an anticipated time-consuming procedure; and the desire to reassess intra-abdominal contents at a directed relook [2]. Overuse of DCS carries its own risks, additional surgeries, ventral hernia, enteroatmospheric fistula, prolonged ventilation, and delayed enteral nutrition, so it should be applied judiciously rather than routinely [3].

Diagnosis

Not covered in source textbooks, damage control surgery is a management strategy rather than a diagnosis; identification of the physiologically compromised patient who requires this strategy is addressed under Scoring and Severity below.

Scoring and Severity

  • Physiological/laboratory criteria for damage control surgery include: core temperature <34°C; pH <7.2; serum lactate >5 mmol/L (normal <2.5 mmol/L); prothrombin time >16 seconds; partial thromboplastin time >60 seconds; more than 10 units of blood transfused; systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg for more than 60 minutes; operating time >60 minutes; and inability to approximate the abdominal incision [2].
  • Venous lactate is a key marker guiding the ETC-versus-DCS decision: <2 mmol/L suggests the patient is resuscitated and suitable for early total care; 2–3 mmol/L requires attention to the trend and other physiological markers; >3 mmol/L suggests under-resuscitation, warranting further resuscitation or DCS if surgery is urgent; and >5 mmol/L indicates DCS [4].
  • Physiological indices supporting suitability for ETC include pulse <100/min, normal blood pressure and respiratory rate, urine output >30 mL/h, absence of hypothermia (temperature ≥35°C), absence of acidosis on arterial blood gas, and a normal coagulation screen [4].
  • The damage control process is staged: Stage I patient selection; Stage II control of haemorrhage and contamination; Stage III resuscitation continued in the ICU; Stage IV definitive surgery; Stage V abdominal closure [2].

Treatment and Management

  • Damage control resuscitation minimizes time in the emergency department, carrying out the majority of resuscitation in the operating theatre rather than the resuscitation bay, individualized through repeated point-of-care testing of haemoglobin, acidosis (pH and lactate), and clotting [2].
  • Hemostatic resuscitation uses red cells, plasma, and platelets in a 1:1:1 ratio to approximate whole blood, with early correction of hypothermia, acidosis, and hypocalcaemia, while crystalloid is minimized (limited to an initial 2 L of lactated Ringer's) to avoid haemodilution [3][5].
  • Permissive hypotension (systolic BP >80 mmHg until haemorrhage is controlled, then target >90 mmHg) is used, except in traumatic brain injury patients, who require a higher initial target (systolic BP >90 mmHg) to preserve cerebral perfusion [5].
  • Tranexamic acid (1 g load, then 1 g over 8 hours) reduces fibrinolysis and bleeding [5].
  • Definitive surgery/anastomoses, vascular reconstruction, and closure of the body cavity should be targeted within 24–72 hours of injury once physiology normalizes, individualized to the patient's response to critical care resuscitation [2].
  • Damage control orthopaedic surgery is limited to debridement of severe open fractures, rapid temporary splintage/stabilisation of long bone fractures, and decompression of limb compartment syndrome, deferring definitive fixation [4].
  • In a multiply injured patient, revascularisation of an injured limb may increase overall threat to life, such that amputation may be the safer option compared with attempted salvage [4].

Surgeries

  • Abdominal damage control laparotomy: midline incision from xiphisternum to pubic symphysis; large clots are evacuated and the abdomen packed in all four quadrants with large swabs to tamponade bleeding; if packing fails to control bleeding, either more packing is needed or a significant arterial source requires direct control (e.g., supracoeliac aortic compression); packs are then removed systematically, one quadrant at a time, to identify the bleeding source, controlled by vessel repair, ligation, organ removal, or repacking [1].
  • Contamination is controlled by primary suture repair of simple bowel injuries or a "clip-and-drop" technique (stapling/tying bowel ends without primary anastomosis) for multiple perforations; bile injuries are managed with a drain; bladder injuries are oversewn with a urethral catheter placed [1].
  • Abbreviated techniques used across the staged process include simple ligation of bleeding vessels, shunting of major arteries and veins, drainage, temporary stapling of bowel, and therapeutic packing [2]. Temporary abdominal closure: achieved with commercial products or a plastic sheet (e.g., OPSITE) over the bowel, an intermediate pack allowing suction, and an outer adherent plastic drape to form a watertight/airtight seal with suction applied to the intermediate layer, the "Vac-Pac" or "OPSITE sandwich" technique [2].
  • In the chest, temporary closure is constructed with chest tubes and a clear drape or Esmarch bandage over the lung, followed by towels, nasogastric tubes, and a large Ioban dressing [3]. Liver packing for major hepatic injury involves perihepatic packing after control of major-vessel bleeding, with patients returning to the OR within 48 hours for pack removal and definitive repair [3]. Damage control vascular surgery uses the largest shunt available that fits the injured vessel to temporize bleeding and restore distal perfusion [3]. Damage control orthopaedics uses external fixators or a pelvic C-clamp for pelvic fractures and external fixators for long bone fractures [3]. Thoracic damage control applies the same philosophy, using the fastest available techniques (e.g., staplers) to control bleeding and limit air leaks while minimizing operative time [2]. Extremity damage control involves shunting of blood vessels and marking/temporizing injuries pending definitive repair [2].
  • Definitive fascial closure is achieved as soon as possible, bearing in mind the risk of abdominal compartment syndrome; if fascial closure cannot be achieved, skin closure alone, or mesh closure with skin grafting and subsequent staged abdominal wall reconstruction, is used [2].

Complications

  • Overuse of damage control surgery can lead to additional surgeries, ventral hernia formation, enteroatmospheric fistula, prolonged mechanical ventilation, and delayed initiation of enteral nutrition, causing significant morbidity, underscoring the need for judicious patient selection [3].
  • Successful abdominal closure after damage control may require aggressive fluid off-loading or even haemofiltration, and closure itself carries risk of abdominal compartment syndrome [2].
  • Tourniquet-type physiological insults, hypothermia (worsened by long operative times, ~2°C/hour), and coagulopathy compound the lethal triad if damage control is delayed inappropriately [2].

Prognosis

In Rotondo's original series, overall survival was similar between damage control and definitive laparotomy groups (55% vs. 58%), but patients with major vascular injury plus two or more visceral injuries had substantially better survival with damage control (77% vs. 11%, P<0.02), establishing the subgroup most likely to benefit [3]. Appropriate, judicious application (not overuse) of damage control principles is associated with improved survival in the unstable, multiply injured patient across the abdomen, chest, pelvis, and extremities [2][3].
