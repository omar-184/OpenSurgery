Abdominal Trauma

Summary

  • Abdominal trauma is a major source of morbidity and mortality after injury, affecting 11.7% of patients in the National Trauma Data Bank with an associated case fatality rate of 12.9%; predominant sources of death are haemorrhage and visceral perforation with sepsis [1].
  • The spleen and liver are the most commonly injured intra-abdominal organs in blunt trauma, while the small bowel is most often injured in penetrating trauma [1][2].
  • Management strategy is dictated primarily by the patient's haemodynamic status rather than injury mechanism alone [1][3].

Definition

Abdominal trauma is classified as blunt (compressive, deceleration, or crush forces causing organ contusion, laceration, or hollow-viscus rupture) or penetrating (stab or gunshot wounds directly lacerating solid or hollow viscera) [1][4]. Patients are further classified by physiological state after initial resuscitation into "normal" (full investigation before treatment), "non-compromised" (limited investigation to determine operative vs non-operative management), and "compromised" (immediate surgery required, investigation suspended) [3].

Pathophysiology

  • Blunt deceleration injury results from differential movement of fixed and non-fixed anatomical structures, causing tearing or avulsion from the vascular pedicle, classically liver tear or vena caval rupture [4].
  • Direct compression bursts solid organs such as the liver when compressed between the impacting object and the ribcage or vertebral column [3].
  • Blunt forces can rupture hollow viscera by rapid compression of a fluid- and gas-filled segment of intestine [1].
  • Most blunt hepatic lacerations occur along segmental fissures because the vascular and biliary structures there are relatively shear-resistant, which explains why a large stellate "bear claw" laceration may produce little intraperitoneal blood [5].
  • Deceleration shear forces can avulse hepatic veins from the vena cava, producing devastating, difficult-to-control haemorrhage [5].
  • High-velocity gunshot wounds transfer greater kinetic energy and cause additional injury through cavitation, tumble, and fragmentation compared with stab wounds or low-velocity gunshot wounds [4].

Clinical features

  • Blood in the peritoneal cavity is not an irritant and initially causes no abdominal pain; distension is subjective and hypotension may be a late sign, particularly in young, fit patients, clinical examination alone is unreliable [3].
  • Kehr sign (referred pain to the left shoulder on deep inspiration) suggests splenic injury with subphrenic irritation [5].
  • Splenic injury classically presents with hypotension, left upper quadrant pain or tenderness, or diffuse peritonitis from extravasated blood [5].
  • An abdominal seat-belt mark or wall ecchymosis raises concern for underlying bowel or mesenteric injury [1][2].
  • Physical examination for liver injury has a false positive rate of approximately 50% and false negative rate of 40% [5].
  • Absolute indications for exploratory laparotomy in penetrating abdominal injury include peritonitis, evisceration, an impaled object, haemodynamic instability, bleeding from a natural orifice, and documented pneumoperitoneum [5].

Etiology

  • Stab wounds commonly involve the liver (40%), small bowel (30%), diaphragm (20%), and colon (15%); high-velocity gunshot wounds commonly involve the small bowel (50%), colon (40%), liver (30%), and vessels (25%) [4].
  • Approximately one-third of stab wounds do not penetrate the peritoneum, and only half of those that do require operative intervention [5].
  • Isolated splenic injury comprises approximately 42% of blunt abdominal injuries, and represented 8.5% of penetrating abdominal injuries in the 2012 NTDB [1].
  • Blunt pancreatic and duodenal injury is uncommon (duodenum <2% of abdominal trauma) because of their retroperitoneal location, with most duodenal injuries due to penetrating mechanisms [1].

Diagnosis

  • FAST (focused assessment with sonography for trauma) examines four windows, subxiphoid (pericardium), left subcostal (splenorenal recess), right subcostal (Morison pouch/hepatorenal), and suprapubic (pelvic cul-de-sac/pouch of Douglas), for free fluid, with a detection threshold of roughly 100–200 mL and reported sensitivity 73–88%, specificity 98–100% [3][4][5]. eFAST is unreliable for excluding injury in penetrating trauma and has low sensitivity (29–35%) for solid organ injury without haemoperitoneum, and cannot reliably assess the retroperitoneum [3].
  • Diagnostic peritoneal lavage (DPL), now largely superseded by FAST and CT, is considered positive with aspiration of >10 mL gross blood, or lavage fluid showing >100,000 RBC/µL or >500 WBC/µL [1][3][5].
  • CT with IV contrast is the gold standard in the haemodynamically stable patient, reliably identifying solid organ injury via disrupted architecture, free fluid, and "vascular blush," but has recognized limitations in detecting hollow viscus and diaphragmatic injury [3][5].
  • Direct CT signs of blunt bowel injury are oral contrast extravasation and free air (present in only 4% and 28% of cases respectively); indirect signs include bowel wall thickening, mesenteric stranding, and unexplained free fluid without solid organ injury [5].
  • Diagnostic laparoscopy is the study of choice for excluding diaphragmatic injury, particularly in left thoracoabdominal penetrating wounds, since no other diagnostic modality can reliably rule this out [1][5].
  • For penetrating flank/back stab wounds, CT with IV (and often rectal) contrast is used; for anterior abdominal stab wounds, local wound exploration, serial abdominal examination, or CT are considered equivalent management pathways in stable, examinable patients [1].

Scoring and Severity

  • The American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) Organ Injury Scale grades splenic, hepatic, and renal injuries (Grades I–V) based on the extent of parenchymal/subcapsular disruption and vascular involvement, and guides operative versus non-operative management [1][3][5].
  • For hepatic injury, Grade I/II injuries constitute 60–70% of cases and are usually managed non-operatively; Grade III injuries occur in ~25%; Grades IV and V occur in 7% and 3% respectively and carry high lethality [5].
  • For bowel injury, the AAST scale separates non-destructive wounds (Grades I–III: contusion up to lacerations >50% circumference without transection, managed by debridement and primary repair) from destructive wounds (Grades IV–V: complete transection, with or without segmental loss/devascularization) [5].
  • Risk factors for failure of non-operative splenic management include higher AAST grade (III–V), large hemoperitoneum, contrast blush/pseudoaneurysm, and possibly age >55 years [5].

Treatment and Management

  • Management is stratified by responder status: "responders" who normalize with resuscitation likely "have bled" rather than continuing to bleed; "non-responders" require immediate intervention; "transient responders" initially improve but relapse and generally require expeditious source control [1].
  • Approximately 60–80% (up to ~90% at high-volume centers) of blunt splenic injuries are managed non-operatively provided the patient has normal vital signs, no peritoneal signs, and no active contrast extravasation; angioembolization for contrast blush/pseudoaneurysm improves non-operative success to 90% or greater and reduces failure rates to ~5% in Grade III–V injuries [1][5].
  • Follow-up CT is recommended 24–48 hours after splenic injury to detect delayed pseudoaneurysm, and at 6 weeks (Grade I–II) or 10–12 weeks (Grade III–V) before return to normal activity [5].
  • Non-operative management of blunt hepatic injury is the treatment approach of choice in the stable patient today [5].
  • Hemodynamic instability mandates expeditious operative management or, if the patient can be stabilized, angiography/embolization [5].
  • Damage control (perihepatic packing, temporary closure) should be considered early in the hypothermic (<32°C), acidotic, coagulopathic patient, and once a patient has required 10 units of transfused blood [5].
  • For hollow viscus injury there is no role for non-operative management [5].
  • Following splenectomy, pneumococcal vaccination (plus Haemophilus influenzae and meningococcal vaccination) should be given, ideally after 14 days post-injury, because of the risk of overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (OPSI) from encapsulated organisms [3][5].

Surgeries

  • Laparotomy technique: midline incision from xiphoid to pubic symphysis; the falciform ligament is divided; blood is evacuated from all four quadrants with packing for temporary haemostasis if the bleeding source is unclear, then systematic exploration from the gastroesophageal junction to the rectum, including the lesser sac [1][5]. Liver injury, the "four Ps": Pressure (bimanual compression), Pringle manoeuvre (clamping the portal triad, safe for up to ~45 minutes) to control hepatic arterial/portal inflow, Plug (silicone tubing or Sengstaken–Blakemore tube for penetrating tracts), and Pack (perihepatic packing for damage control) [3][5].
  • The finger-fracture technique exposes bleeding vessels within a parenchymal wound for direct ligation or clipping; an omental patch sutured over a raw liver surface provides haemostasis and internal drainage (~90% success) [5].
  • The hepatic artery may be ligated if injured, but the portal vein must be repaired or shunted (ligation carries >50% mortality) [3].
  • Anatomic hepatic resection for trauma has been largely abandoned in favor of resectional debridement of non-viable tissue [5].
  • Closed suction drainage is left after hepatic surgery [3]. Splenic injury, splenectomy is the safer option in the haemodynamically compromised or multiply injured patient; splenorrhaphy (partial resection with omental buttress) may be feasible in stable patients with limited parenchymal injury, preserving roughly 50% of splenic mass to maintain immunologic function [3][5]. Pancreatic injury, distal injuries (left of the superior mesenteric vessels) are treated with closed drainage ± distal pancreatectomy if the duct is involved; proximal injuries are managed as conservatively as possible; pancreaticoduodenectomy (Whipple) is rarely indicated emergently because of very high associated mortality [3]. Biliary injury, the common bile duct can be repaired over a T-tube, drained, or ligated as part of damage control [3].

Complications

  • Missed hollow viscus injury is the "nemesis" of non-operative management of blunt abdominal trauma, given its catastrophic consequences if delayed [5].
  • Delayed splenic rupture may occur 6–8 days after injury, particularly with subcapsular hematoma, which is at increased risk because oozing from the raw parenchymal surface can progressively disrupt the capsule; overall delayed rupture incidence after nonoperative management is low (~1.4%) [5].
  • Splenic embolization for subcapsular hematoma is less effective and can cause pain and abscess formation from main splenic artery coiling [5].
  • Overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (OPSI), caused by encapsulated organisms (Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, Neisseria meningitidis), carries reported incidence of 0.9% and mortality 0.8% in adults after splenectomy for all causes, with children at greater risk [5][7].
  • Concomitant hollow viscus injury occurs in 5–20% of major hepatic injuries [5].
  • Common post-splenectomy complications include subdiaphragmatic abscess, pancreatic tail injury, and gastric perforation from short gastric vessel ligation [7].

Prognosis

Blunt abdominal trauma overall mortality is reported at 10–30% [8]. Higher AAST injury grade (Grade IV/V) hepatic injury carries high lethality [5]. Nonoperative management of splenic and hepatic injury reduces hospital length of stay, transfusion requirements, and non-therapeutic laparotomy rates without increasing mortality when properly selected [5].
