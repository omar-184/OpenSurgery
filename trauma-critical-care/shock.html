Shock

Summary

  • Shock is defined as inadequate end-organ perfusion and tissue oxygenation; "cellular shock" describes the resulting failure of normal cellular processes, including oxygen processing [1].
  • Hemorrhage is the most common cause of shock in the injured patient, but non-hemorrhagic causes (tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, neurogenic, septic) must be actively considered [2].
  • Shock occurs at three conceptual levels of the cardiovascular system: the heart (cardiogenic, extrinsic or intrinsic), the large/medium vessels (hemorrhagic), and the small vessels (neurogenic or septic vasodilation and maldistribution) [3].

Definition

Shock is inadequate perfusion of tissue relative to metabolic demand; blood delivers oxygen along with countless nutrients, buffers, hormones, and electrolytes, so oxygen delivery alone is an oversimplified way to conceptualize the problem [3]. Types of shock, by underlying haemodynamic profile, are: hemorrhagic (hypovolemic), low CVP/PCWP, low cardiac output, high systemic vascular resistance (SVR); septic (hyperdynamic), low CVP/PCWP (usually), high cardiac output, low SVR; cardiogenic (e.g., MI, cardiac tamponade), high CVP/PCWP, low cardiac output, high SVR; neurogenic (e.g., head or spinal cord injury) (low CVP/PCWP, low cardiac output, low SVR; and adrenal insufficiency) low CVP/PCWP (usually), low cardiac output, low SVR [4].

Pathophysiology

  • In neurogenic shock, loss of sympathetic tone causes vasodilation; in sepsis, systemic vascular resistance falls because of a dysregulated inflammatory/homeostatic response [3].
  • The neurohormonal response to hypovolemia has a rapid component (epinephrine/norepinephrine release causing vasoconstriction and increased cardiac activity) and a sustained component (renin-angiotensin activation causing vasoconstriction and water resorption, ADH release causing water reabsorption, and ACTH release increasing cortisol) [5].
  • Arterial bleeding often stops temporarily via vessel spasm and thrombosis in adventitial tissue, but a lacerated (versus transected) artery can bleed more because spasm enlarges the defect; since arteries lack valves, blood pressure can fall early, before large-volume loss, so hypotension may precede tissue ischemia and lactate/base deficit may remain normal [3].
  • Venous bleeding is typically slower, allowing time for fluid recruitment from intracellular/interstitial spaces, so large volumes can be lost before hypotension occurs, but tissue ischemia (abnormal lactate/base deficit) develops during this process [3].
  • Children compensate for large blood loss (higher body water composition) often with only tachycardia until an abrupt decompensation point; elderly patients decompensate at lower blood loss volumes because of reduced cardiac compensation and fluid reserve [3].

Clinical features

  • General recognition of shock: decreased blood pressure, increased pulse, cold/clammy/pale/sweating skin, and confusion (agitated or drowsy); young patients compensate, showing only decreased pulse pressure, tachycardia, and decreased urine output [1].
  • Hypovolemic shock arises from trauma, ruptured AAA, ruptured ectopic pregnancy, postoperative hemorrhage, dehydration, burns, or pancreatitis [1].
  • Anaphylactic shock presents with sudden onset after drug/blood product/latex exposure, with stridor or bronchospasm, angio-oedema, urticaria, and pruritus [1].
  • Septic shock may mimic hypovolemic shock, or in its earlier evolution present with a "septic" look (pyrexial, flushed, bounding pulses) before circulatory collapse [1].
  • Cardiogenic shock presents with a history of recent surgery/trauma, chest pain, dyspnoea, palpitations, or new cardiac murmurs [1].
  • Heart rate above 100 beats/min has traditionally been used as a sign of bleeding, but evidence shows it is neither sensitive nor specific for the need for emergent intervention or transfusion; "relative bradycardia" (heart rate <100 with SBP <90 mmHg) occurs in a substantial proportion of hypotensive bleeding patients and is associated with lower mortality down to a heart rate of about 60 bpm [3].

Etiology

  • The most common cause of shock in the injured patient is hemorrhage [2].
  • Non-hemorrhagic causes of shock in trauma include tension pneumothorax, neurogenic/spinal injury, cardiac tamponade, cardiac contusion, sepsis (from delayed presentation), and nontraumatic causes such as pre-existing myocardial infarction or stroke that precipitated the injury [2].
  • Rapidly reversible causes of cardiogenic shock include cardiac tamponade, arrhythmias, and tension pneumothorax; other causes include fluid overload/congestive heart failure, myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism, endocarditis, aortic dissection, and decompensated valvular disease [1].

Diagnosis

  • Blood pressure remains the most clinically useful monitoring measure for shock, though a combination of normalization of serum lactate, base deficit, and pH along with haemorrhage control are useful resuscitation endpoints [3].
  • A low haemoglobin/haematocrit generally reflects severe blood loss when it occurs rapidly, but a normal or high level on initial presentation does not rule out significant bleeding, particularly before crystalloid administration [3].
  • The Shock Index (SI = heart rate ÷ systolic blood pressure) is a better marker of shock severity than heart rate or blood pressure alone, and is useful across trauma, sepsis, obstetric, and cardiac aetiologies; a Modified Shock Index (heart rate ÷ mean arterial pressure) additionally accounts for diastolic pressure and better predicts mortality [3].
  • Hypotension has traditionally been defined as systolic BP ≤90 mmHg, though redefinition below 110 mmHg has been proposed, since a prehospital BP below 110 mmHg is associated with an inflection point in mortality [3].
  • Early gram-negative sepsis shows decreased insulin and increased glucose (impaired utilization); late gram-negative sepsis shows increased insulin and increased glucose (insulin resistance); hyperglycaemia often precedes clinical sepsis [5].
  • Procalcitonin is elevated in sepsis (higher sensitivity, lower specificity), useful for ruling out sepsis and for guiding antibiotic discontinuation when it normalizes; serial lactate is used to guide volume resuscitation (target <2.0) [5].

Scoring and Severity

  • The ATLS classification defines four classes of haemorrhagic shock by percentage blood loss: Class I (0–15%, slightly anxious, pulse <100, normal BP, urine >30 mL/h, treat with crystalloid); Class II (15–30%, mildly anxious, pulse >100, normal systolic BP but decreased pulse pressure, urine 20–30 mL/h, crystalloid); Class III (30–40%, anxious/confused, pulse >120, decreased BP, urine 5–15 mL/h, crystalloid plus blood); Class IV (>40%, confused/lethargic, pulse >140, decreased BP, negligible urine output, crystalloid plus blood) [3].
  • These four classes are explicitly noted to be arbitrarily generated and not rigorously validated, patients, especially at the extremes of age, often do not exhibit the full described pattern [3].
  • Class III shock (30–40% loss) equates to roughly 2 litres of blood loss in an average 75 kg male [3].
  • SIRS criteria include temperature >38°C or <36°C, heart rate >90/min, respiratory rate >20/min or PaCO2 <32, and white blood count >12,000/µL or <4,000/µL; shock is defined as arterial hypotension despite adequate volume resuscitation; multi-organ dysfunction (MOD) is progressive but reversible dysfunction of two or more organs from acute disruption of homeostasis [5].

Treatment and Management

  • General emergency management: seek help early, secure the airway with high-flow oxygen if patent, check central pulses, secure IV access, give a rapid 500 mL crystalloid challenge, then differentiate the type of shock [1]. Hypovolaemic shock: lie the patient flat with high-flow oxygen, elevate the legs if no IV access, repeat rapid 500 mL fluid boluses watching for BP response, send bloods (FBC, U&E, clotting, cross-match) and arterial blood gas [1]. Anaphylactic shock: sit the patient up, give high-flow oxygen, 0.5 mL of 1:1000 adrenaline IM repeated every 5 minutes if no improvement, hydrocortisone 100 mg and chlorphenamine 10 mg IV, nebulised salbutamol if wheezy [1]. Septic shock: treat as for hypovolaemic shock, add vasopressors if fluid-replete, take blood cultures before starting empirical broad-spectrum antibiotics [1][5]. Cardiogenic shock: high-flow oxygen, IV morphine, cardiac monitoring and 12-lead ECG, treat arrhythmias per ALS algorithm, treat myocardial infarction (aspirin, clopidogrel, LMWH/fondaparinux if not contraindicated), treat tension pneumothorax or tamponade specifically, consider diuretics for fluid overload, and echocardiography to assess for effusion/valvular disease and LV function [1].
  • Damage control resuscitation for hemorrhagic shock uses balanced blood product transfusion (1:1:1), minimizes crystalloid, and pursues rapid haemorrhage control [2].
  • Vasoactive/inotropic agents used across shock states are titrated by receptor pharmacology: dopamine (renal-dose 2–5 µg/kg/min, beta-adrenergic 6–10, alpha-adrenergic >10), dobutamine (beta-1, increases contractility), norepinephrine (alpha-1/2 with some beta-1, potent splanchnic vasoconstrictor), phenylephrine (pure alpha-1), and vasopressin (V1 arterial vasoconstriction, V2 renal water reabsorption) [5].
  • An intra-aortic balloon pump (inflates in diastole, deflates in systole) is used in cardiogenic shock to reduce afterload and improve diastolic coronary perfusion, contraindicated in aortic dissection, severe aortoiliac disease, and aortic regurgitation [5].

Surgeries

Definitive surgical haemorrhage control (laparotomy, thoracotomy, angioembolization) is indicated when shock fails to respond to volume resuscitation, since ongoing bleeding (not further fluid) is the underlying problem [3][7]. For cardiac tamponade causing obstructive/cardiogenic shock, pericardial window or pericardiocentesis is required; a coding post-cardiac-surgery patient with tamponade requires bedside sternal re-opening in the ICU (cutting wires, chest spreader) [5]. Not covered in source textbooks beyond the procedures addressed in the Damage Control Surgery, Abdominal Trauma, and Thoracic Trauma topic pages.

Complications

  • Massive haemorrhage and its resuscitation predispose to the "lethal triad" of hypothermia, coagulopathy, and acidosis [2][8].
  • Prolonged or severe shock leads to multi-organ dysfunction (MOD), a progressive but potentially reversible failure of two or more organ systems [5].
  • Excessive positive end-expiratory pressure and over-aggressive crystalloid resuscitation can themselves worsen venous return, cardiac output, and pulmonary status during shock management [2][5].

Prognosis

  • Bleeding patients with a heart rate of 60–90 beats/min (relative bradycardia) have the highest survival compared with tachycardic patients, though a heart rate below 60 bpm is usually a sign of a moribund patient [3].
  • A prehospital systolic blood pressure below 110 mmHg is associated with a measurable inflection point in mortality, with roughly 15% of such patients eventually dying in hospital [3].
  • Septic shock carries a high mortality; earlier recognition (elevated lactate, procalcitonin trends) and prompt source control/antibiotics improve outcomes [1][5].
