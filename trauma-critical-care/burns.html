Burns

Summary

  • Burns cause a multisystem injury, though the skin is by far the most commonly affected organ; major determinants of outcome are the percentage of total body surface area (TBSA) burned, presence of inhalation injury, burn depth, and patient age/comorbidities [1].
  • Above 15% TBSA in adults (10% in children), the inflammatory fluid shift produces burn shock requiring formal fluid resuscitation [1].
  • Scald burns are the most common mechanism overall, while flame burns are more likely to result in hospital admission [2].

Definition

Burns are classified by degree: first-degree (epidermis only, e.g. sunburn); second-degree, subdivided into superficial (papillary) dermis (painful, blisters, hair follicles intact, blanches, does not need grafting) and deep (reticular) dermis, decreased sensation, loss of hair follicles, needs grafting; third-degree (full thickness, leathery/charred, down to subcutaneous fat); and fourth-degree (down to bone, muscle, or adjacent adipose tissue) [2]. Bailey & Love's functional classification groups burns as "Group A" (superficial enough to heal spontaneously within 14 days with good functional/cosmetic outcome) versus "Group B" (deep enough to require prolonged secondary-intention healing or surgical closure) [1].

Pathophysiology

  • Burn injury triggers release of neuropeptides and complement activation from heat-altered proteins and pain fibre stimulation; activation of Hageman factor initiates protease cascades affecting arachidonic acid, thrombin, and kallikrein pathways, producing vastly increased vascular permeability with fluid, solute, and protein loss from the intravascular to extravascular space, proportional to burn area [1].
  • Cell-mediated immunity is significantly reduced in large burns, increasing susceptibility to bacterial/fungal infection; granulocyte chemotaxis is also impaired [1][2].
  • Gut mucosal ischaemia reduces motility and increases bacterial translocation, and can contribute to abdominal compartment syndrome [1].
  • Circumferential full-thickness burns act as a tourniquet on limbs (ischaemia) or a mechanical restriction on the chest wall (impaired ventilation) as the burned, inelastic skin resists swelling [1].
  • Carbon monoxide binds haemoglobin ~250 times more avidly than oxygen, forming inactive carboxyhaemoglobin, impairing oxygen delivery, and inhibiting cytochrome oxidase; it also falsely elevates pulse oximetry readings and shifts the oxygen-haemoglobin dissociation curve leftward [1][3].
  • Cyanide from burning nitrogen-containing polymers disrupts electron transport at cytochrome A3, preventing oxygen utilisation [1][3].

Clinical features

  • Warning signs of airway/inhalation injury include facial and neck burns, blistering inside the mouth, being trapped in an enclosed space, hoarseness/voice change, stridor, and singeing of facial/nasal hair [1].
  • Superficial partial-thickness burns show blistering, pink moist dermis, brisk capillary refill on blanching, and normal pinprick sensation; deep partial-thickness burns show fixed capillary staining that does not blanch and reduced sensation (unable to distinguish sharp/blunt); full-thickness burns are hard and leathery, show no capillary return, and are completely anaesthetic [1].
  • Inhalational injury symptoms (progressive respiratory effort, tachycardia, anxiety, confusion, falling saturations) may take 24 hours to 5 days to develop [1].
  • Signs suggesting burn wound infection include peripheral oedema, conversion of a second- to third-degree burn, haemorrhage into the scar, erythema gangrenosum, green fat, black skin discoloration around the wound, and rapid eschar separation [2].

Etiology

  • Scalds tend to be superficial (deep in infants/elderly without good first aid); flame burns are mixed deep dermal/full thickness; alkali burns (including cement) are often deep dermal or full thickness via liquefaction necrosis; acid burns cause coagulation necrosis and are superficial at weak concentrations, deep at strong concentrations; electrical contact burns are typically full thickness and always deeper than they appear externally [1][2].
  • Risk factors for burn injury include alcohol/drug use, extremes of age, smoking, low socioeconomic status, violence, and epilepsy [2].
  • Non-accidental injury accounts for 15% of paediatric burns; suspicious features include delayed presentation, conflicting histories, sharply demarcated margins, uniform depth, absence of splash marks, stocking/glove patterns, flexor sparing, and dorsal hand involvement [2].

Diagnosis

  • Burn size is estimated using the Wallace rule of nines (each arm 9%, head 9%, each leg 18%, anterior and posterior torso 18% each, genitalia 1%) or the patient's palm (≈1% TBSA) for small burns; the Lund and Browder chart is the standard, more accurate tool for larger burns and adjusts for the proportionally larger head and smaller legs in children [1][2].
  • Burn depth assessment uses history (temperature/duration of exposure), blanching, capillary refill, and sensation, following a stepwise protocol (Nikolsky sign, blister type, colour, sensation) [1].
  • Inhalation injury is suspected from history and confirmed by fibreoptic bronchoscopy; chest radiograph may show patchy consolidation and soot may be visible in the nose/oropharynx [1][2].
  • Burn wound infection is best diagnosed by quantitative wound biopsy (distinguishing infection, >10^5 organisms, from colonization) [2].

Scoring and Severity

  • Admission-to-burns-unit criteria include suspected airway/inhalational injury, any burn likely to need fluid resuscitation or surgery, burns of significance to hands/face/feet/perineum, extremes of age, non-accidental injury, and high-tension electrical or concentrated hydrofluoric acid burns [1].
  • ABSITE Review criteria: second/third-degree burns >10% BSA in patients <10 or >50 years, >20% BSA in other adults, third-degree burns >5% BSA at any age, burns to hands/face/feet/genitalia/perineum/major joints, electrical/chemical burns, and inhalational injury [2].
  • Intravenous fluid resuscitation is indicated for adult burns >15% TBSA and paediatric burns >10% TBSA [1], or per ABSITE Review, the (modified) Parkland formula is used for burns ≥20% BSA of at least second degree, capped at 50% BSA for calculation purposes [2].
  • Adequate resuscitation is judged by urine output of 0.5–1.0 mL/kg/h in adults (>1 mL/kg/h in children, >2 mL/kg/h in infants <6 months); output >2 mL/kg/h should prompt a reduction in infusion rate [1][2].
  • Abnormal carboxyhaemoglobin is >10% (>20% in smokers) [3].

Treatment and Management

  • Prehospital: ensure rescuer safety, stop the burning process, cool the wound with tepid (~15°C) water for at least 20 minutes (effective up to 1 hour post-injury, while avoiding hypothermia), give oxygen, elevate burned limbs/sit patient up for airway burns, and provide analgesia [1]. Hospital management follows ATLS with an added "F" for fluid resuscitation: Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure, Fluid resuscitation [1]. Airway: early elective intubation is safest for suspected airway burn because delay allows swelling to make intubation difficult or impossible (window of occlusion typically 4–24 hours); cricothyroidotomy equipment must be available [1].
  • Indications for intubation include upper airway stridor/obstruction, worsening hypoxaemia, or anticipated massive volume resuscitation [2]. Fluid resuscitation: the modified Parkland formula (4 mL × %TBSA burn × weight (kg)) with half given over the first 8 hours and the remainder over the next 16 hours, using lactated Ringer's/Hartmann's solution; colloid (albumin) in the first 24 hours increases pulmonary complications and should be reserved for after 24 hours; children also require maintenance fluid [1][2].
  • The Parkland formula can significantly underestimate requirements with inhalational injury, alcohol intoxication, electrical injury, or post-escharotomy [2].
  • Hypothermia, part of the lethal triad, is prevented with warmed fluids, external warming devices, and increased ambient temperature [1]. Carbon monoxide poisoning is treated with high-flow, high-concentration (100%) oxygen, rarely hyperbaric oxygen [1][3]. Cyanide poisoning is treated with intravenous hydroxycobalamin (vitamin B12), or amyl/sodium nitrite [1][3]. Wound dressings for Group A (superficial) burns include silver sulfadiazine, hydrocolloids (e.g., Duoderm), biosynthetic dressings (e.g., Biobrane), and silver nanocrystal dressings (e.g., Acticoat) [1]. Nutrition: caloric need = 25 kcal/kg/day + (30 kcal × %burn); protein need = 1 g/kg/day + (3 g × %burn); glucose is the preferred non-protein calorie source; enteral feeding tubes are placed for significant BSA burns [2]. Special burns: acid/alkali burns require copious water irrigation (alkalis cause deeper injury via liquefaction necrosis than acids, which cause coagulation necrosis); hydrofluoric acid burns are treated with topical/local calcium; powder burns are wiped away before irrigation; tar burns are cooled then removed with a lipophilic solvent [2].
  • Electrical burns require cardiac monitoring, as internal injury is always worse than skin findings suggest, with risk of rhabdomyolysis, compartment syndrome, and delayed neurological complications [2].
  • Tetanus prophylaxis is required for burn wounds [2].
  • There is no role for prophylactic systemic antibiotics in burns [2].

Surgeries

  • Escharotomy is required for circumferential full-thickness burns to limbs (tourniquet-like ischaemia) or torso (mechanical ventilatory restriction), performed within 4–6 hours of injury; limb incisions run in the midaxial line avoiding major nerves and vessels (anterior/medial to the elbow to avoid the ulnar nerve; posterior/medial to the ankle to avoid the long saphenous vein; anterior to the fibular head to avoid the common peroneal nerve), and chest escharotomy uses two longitudinal incisions (lateral to the nipples) joined by horizontal incisions below the clavicles and at the xiphisternum [1][2].
  • Fasciotomy may be additionally required if compartment syndrome is suspected after escharotomy [2]. Burn excision: early total excision (as soon as the patient is stabilized) exploits the "anaesthetic," "haemodynamic," and "bacterial" windows to reduce pulmonary decompensation, blood loss, and infection risk; a staged approach with serial debridement is used where resources are limited [1].
  • Wounds should generally be excised within 72 hours (after adequate fluid resuscitation); viability during excision is judged primarily by punctate bleeding, then colour and texture, using a dermatome [2].
  • Each excision session should aim for <1 L blood loss, <20% of skin excised, and <2 hours operative time [2].
  • Face, palms, soles, and genital burns are typically deferred for the first week [2]. Grafting: autografts (split-thickness [STSG] or full-thickness [FTSG]) are preferred over allografts/homografts (last ~4 weeks before rejection), xenografts (porcine, ~2 weeks, do not vascularize), or dermal substitutes; graft take proceeds via imbibition (days 0–3) then neovascularization (from day 3) [2].
  • Skin grafting is contraindicated over poorly vascularized beds (tendon, bone without periosteum, irradiated tissue) or when wound culture shows beta-haemolytic strep or >10^5 organisms [2].
  • STSGs survive more reliably (thinner, easier imbibition); FTSGs cause less contraction and are preferred for palms and dorsal hands (immobilized in extension for 7 days post-graft) [2].
  • Meshed grafts are used on the back, flank, trunk, and limbs; genital burns can use meshed STSG [2].
  • Infected burn wounds are treated by excision with allograft (not autograft) placement plus systemic antibiotics [2].

Complications

  • Carbon monoxide poisoning is the most common immediate cause of fire-related death [1].
  • Pneumonia is the most common infection and the most common cause of death in patients with >30% BSA burns, with inhalational injury the number-one risk factor [2].
  • Burn wound infection risk rises with burn size (more common >30% BSA); Pseudomonas is the classically cited most common organism, followed by Staphylococcus, E. coli, and Enterobacter; HSV is the most common viral infection of burn wounds [2].
  • Silver sulfadiazine can cause neutropenia/thrombocytopenia and has limited eschar penetration; silver nitrate causes electrolyte imbalance (hyponatraemia, hypochloremia, hypocalcaemia, hypokalaemia) and methemoglobinemia (contraindicated in G6PD deficiency); mafenide acetate (Sulfamylon) has the broadest antipseudomonal/Gram-negative coverage and best eschar penetration but causes metabolic acidosis via carbonic anhydrase inhibition [2].
  • The most common reason for skin graft loss is seroma or haematoma formation beneath the graft, preventing attachment (mitigated with a pressure/bolster dressing) [2].
  • Extremely deep burns, electrical burns, or compartment syndrome can cause rhabdomyolysis with myoglobinuria, treated with hydration and urine alkalinization [2].
  • Electrical burns carry risk of polyneuritis, quadriplegia, transverse myelitis, cataracts, liver necrosis, intestinal/gallbladder perforation, pancreatic necrosis, and posterior shoulder dislocation/vertebral fracture from tetanic contraction [2].
  • Ectropion from burn contracture of the eyelids is treated with eyelid release [2].

Prognosis

  • Superficial partial-thickness (Group A) burns heal without residual scarring within about 2 weeks; deep dermal burns take 3 weeks or more without surgery and usually lead to hypertrophic scarring [1].
  • Mortality from burns is highest in children and the elderly, related to difficulty escaping the fire [2].
  • Full-thickness burns virtually always require excision and grafting except for the very smallest [1].
