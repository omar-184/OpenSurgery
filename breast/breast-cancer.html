Breast Cancer

Summary

  • Breast cancer is the most frequent cancer among women worldwide, with an estimated 2.3 million new cases diagnosed in 2020, and it is the second leading cause of cancer death in females in the United States [1][2].
  • It arises predominantly from the ductal epithelium (about 90% of cases) and less commonly from the lobule, progressing from in-situ to invasive disease once tumour cells breach the ductal/lobular basement membrane [1].
  • Diagnosis relies on triple assessment (clinical examination, imaging, and tissue sampling), and treatment is multimodal, combining surgery, radiotherapy and systemic therapy tailored to tumour stage and biology [1][2].
  • Five-year survival has improved steadily, from 63% in the early 1960s to 91% during 2012–2018 in the United States, reflecting earlier detection and improved therapy [2].

Definition

  • Breast carcinoma arises from the milk ducts in 90% (ductal carcinoma) or from the lobule in 10% (lobular carcinoma) of patients; disease confined to the epithelium without breach of the basement membrane is termed in-situ disease, while infiltration through a breach in the basement membrane produces invasive (infiltrating) ductal or lobular carcinoma [1].
  • Noninvasive (in-situ) breast neoplasms were previously divided into lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS) and ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS); LCIS is no longer regarded as a neoplasm in the 8th edition AJCC staging system but rather as a risk factor for future breast cancer [2].
  • Invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC), also called infiltrating ductal carcinoma or "no special type" (NST), is the most common form of breast cancer, accounting for 50–80% of invasive cancers, while invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) accounts for 5–15% [1][2].
  • Paget disease of the nipple, first described by Paget in 1874, accounts for 1% or less of breast malignancies and is characterised histologically by malignant Paget cells within the nipple epidermis without invasion through the dermal basement membrane [2].
  • Inflammatory breast cancer is a purely clinical diagnosis, defined by diffuse erythema and oedema (peau d'orange) involving more than one-third of the breast, caused by tumour cell plugging of dermal lymphatics, and is the most aggressive subtype, accounting for 1–5% of all breast tumours [1][2].

Pathophysiology

  • Invasive breast cancers lack organised glandular architecture, infiltrating haphazardly into stroma; pathologists broadly divide invasive breast cancer into ductal and lobular histologic types, with IDC tending to grow as a cohesive mass detectable on mammography and ILC permeating the breast in a single-file pattern that often escapes detection, characterised by CDH1 mutation causing loss of E-cadherin [2].
  • DCIS is recognised as discrete duct spaces filled with malignant cells contained by a basement membrane, with five morphologic subtypes (micropapillary, papillary, cribriform, solid, and comedo); central necrosis of DCIS cells calcifies to produce the pleomorphic microcalcifications visible on mammography, and untreated DCIS can progress to invasive cancer, usually recapitulating the morphology of the in-situ component [2].
  • Locally, the tumour invades adjacent breast parenchyma and may involve skin or chest wall; tumour cells release fibroblast growth factor (FGF), transforming growth factors (TGFα/β) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and FGF induces a desmoplastic reaction in which fibroblasts lay down collagen, whose contraction shortens Cooper's ligaments to produce dimpling, puckering/tethering, or nipple retraction [1].
  • Lymphatic spread occurs mainly to axillary lymph nodes, with 70–80% of lymphatic flow from the breast directed to the axilla, 2–3% to the internal mammary nodes alone, and about 20% to both; tumours from the inner half of the breast may also drain to internal mammary nodes [1][2].
  • Haematogenous metastasis follows the onset of neoangiogenesis at a tumour size of 1–2 mm and occurs most commonly to the skeletal system (lumbar vertebrae, femoral neck, thoracic vertebrae, rib, skull), and also to liver, lungs, brain, and occasionally adrenal glands and ovaries [1].
  • Tumour grade is assessed using the Nottingham Histologic Score (a modification of Bloom–Richardson), summing three components (glandular/tubular formation, nuclear pleomorphism, and mitotic rate, each scored 1–3) to yield grade 1 (score 3–5, well differentiated), grade 2 (6–7, moderately differentiated), or grade 3 (8–9, poorly differentiated) [1][2].
  • Molecular subtyping by gene-expression profiling identifies four to five clusters, luminal A, luminal B, HER2-enriched, basal-like (triple negative), and a claudin-low/normal-like group, correlating with ER/PR and HER2 status, grade, and Ki-67 proliferation index [1][2].

Clinical features

  • A discrete, painless lump is the most common presentation, most frequently in the upper outer quadrant of the breast; other features include nipple retraction, nipple discharge (blood or serous), skin ulceration, peau d'orange, satellite nodules, and skin dimpling or tethering [1].
  • Peau d'orange results from obstruction of cutaneous lymphatic drainage by tumour infiltration of subdermal lymphatics or axillary nodes and is a sign of locally advanced disease; cancer en cuirasse describes extensive tumour infiltration of the skin of the breast, chest, upper limb and abdomen [1].
  • On examination, carcinoma is typically firm, less circumscribed than a benign lesion, and moving it produces drag of adjacent tissue, in contrast to fibroadenomas and cysts, which are well circumscribed and mobile [2].
  • Paget disease presents with nipple erythema, irritation and pruritus that may progress to crusting and ulceration, originating on the nipple and secondarily spreading to the areola (unlike eczema, which begins on the areola); a palpable mass accompanies Paget disease in slightly more than 50% of patients [2].
  • Inflammatory breast cancer manifests as erythema, oedema, and warmth of the breast due to lymphatic obstruction, often without a discrete underlying mass, and has a rapid onset over weeks to three months [2].
  • Locally advanced disease may present with a large primary tumour (>5 cm), chest wall involvement, skin ulceration or satellite nodules, or bulky/fixed axillary nodes [2].
  • Symptoms of metastatic disease include bone pain, cough, breathlessness, haemoptysis, headache, visual disturbance, neurological deficit, seizures, abdominal distension, jaundice, anorexia, weakness, weight loss and hypercalcaemia [1].
  • In males, most present with a subareolar breast mass, with nipple involvement in up to 50% of cases [2].

Etiology

  • Age is probably the most important risk factor, with incidence increasing with age and a cumulative lifetime incidence of 12.9% [2].
  • Female sex is a major risk factor, as males account for less than 1% of breast cancer cases [1][2].
  • Modifiable risk factors include obesity (RR 1.29 postmenopausally), nulliparity or first pregnancy after age 35, shorter duration of breastfeeding, use of hormone replacement therapy for more than 10 years (RR 1.2), tobacco use, alcohol consumption (RR up to 1.46 for heavy drinking), and prior chest wall radiation (RR 6, especially before age 30, e.g. for Hodgkin lymphoma) [1][2].
  • Reproductive/hormonal risk factors that increase lifetime oestrogen exposure include early menarche (before age 11, RR 1.19), late menopause (after age 55, RR 1.12), and nulliparity [1][2].
  • Family history confers a 1.5-fold increased risk with one affected first-degree relative and a two- to four-fold excess risk with more than one; genetic and familial factors account for about 10% of all breast cancer cases [1][2].
  • BRCA1 (17q21) confers a lifetime breast cancer risk of over 60% (50–85%) and up to 40–58% ovarian cancer risk, with BRCA1-associated cancers usually triple-negative; BRCA2 (13q12.3) confers a similar breast cancer risk with more commonly hormone-receptor-positive tumours, and is also associated with cancers of the prostate, pancreas, and, more than BRCA1, male breast cancer [1][2].
  • Additional high-penetrance genes include TP53 (Li-Fraumeni syndrome), PTEN (Cowden syndrome), STK11 (Peutz-Jeghers syndrome), PALB2, ATM, CHEK2, and CDH1 [1][2].
  • High-risk histologic lesions include proliferative disease without atypia (RR 1.3–1.9), proliferative disease with atypia, atypical ductal or lobular hyperplasia (RR 3.7–5), and classic LCIS (RR 7–10), which is regarded as a risk marker rather than a cancer precursor [1][2].
  • In males, risk factors include increasing age, radiation exposure, testicular disease, obesity, cirrhosis, Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY), family history, and BRCA2 mutations; gynecomastia is not a risk factor [2].

Diagnosis

  • Patients presenting with a breast lump, nipple discharge, or other breast symptoms are assessed using "triple assessment", clinical history and examination, radiological imaging (ultrasonography and/or mammography), and tissue sampling (cytology or histology), an approach with a diagnostic accuracy approaching 100% [1].
  • Screening mammography is performed in asymptomatic women to detect occult cancer; guidelines recommend starting at age 40 in most US society guidelines, with the UK NHS inviting women aged 50–70 every three years, and population-based mammographic screening achieving 90–95% long-term survival in screen-detected tumours [1][2].
  • The Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS), developed by the American College of Radiology, standardises the degree of suspicion of malignancy on mammography, ultrasound or MRI on a 0–6 scale, guiding the decision for biopsy [2].
  • Ultrasonography is the primary imaging modality in young women with dense breast tissue, distinguishing cystic from solid lesions, while solid masses with irregular shape and ill-defined (indistinct, angular, or spiculated) margins are suspicious for malignancy and require biopsy [1].
  • Core-needle biopsy (CNB) is the method of choice for tissue diagnosis of breast lesions, performed under mammographic (stereotactic), ultrasound, or MRI guidance, and can be complemented by fine-needle aspiration (FNA) of suspicious axillary lymph nodes, which has a reported sensitivity of approximately 90% [2].
  • MRI is used selectively, for identifying an unknown primary in patients with axillary metastases, assessing extent of disease in dense breasts, evaluating residual disease after lumpectomy with positive margins, or screening high-risk patients, although no high-level evidence shows that MRI-guided decision-making improves local recurrence or survival [2].
  • Contrast-enhanced CT of the chest, abdomen and pelvis and an isotope bone scan are used for metastatic work-up in locally advanced disease (T3, T4, N2, N3), whereas patients with early breast cancer (T1–T2, N0–N1) require metastatic evaluation only if symptomatic or with raised alkaline phosphatase; PET-CT may also be used [1].
  • Routinely reported biomarkers are oestrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and HER2, assessed by immunohistochemistry (HER2 scored 0 to 3+, with equivocal 2+ results reflexed to in-situ hybridisation), along with the Ki-67 proliferation index [2].
  • Genomic assays such as the 21-gene Oncotype DX Recurrence Score, MammaPrint (70-gene), PAM50/Prosigna, and EndoPredict help predict benefit from chemotherapy in ER-positive, HER2-negative, node-negative or limited node-positive disease [1][2].

Scoring and Severity

  • Breast cancer staging uses the tumour-node-metastasis (TNM) classification, currently in its 8th AJCC/UICC edition, which incorporates T (primary tumour size and chest wall/skin involvement), N (regional lymph node status), and M (distant metastasis) categories along with histologic grade, ER/PR/HER2 status, and genomic assay results to refine prognosis [1][2].
  • T categories range from Tis (DCIS or Paget disease without invasion) through T1 (≤20 mm, with subdivisions T1mi ≤1 mm, T1a >1–5 mm, T1b >5–10 mm, T1c >10–20 mm), T2 (>20–50 mm), T3 (>50 mm), to T4 (any size with chest wall or skin extension; T4d denotes inflammatory carcinoma) [1].
  • N categories range from cN0 (no nodal metastases) through cN1 (mobile ipsilateral level I–II axillary nodes) to cN3 (infraclavicular, internal mammary with axillary, or supraclavicular node involvement); M0 denotes no distant metastases and cM1 denotes clinically or radiographically detected distant metastases [1].
  • Clinical (cTNM) and pathologic (pTNM) staging are distinguished, with the "y" prefix denoting post-neoadjuvant status (ypTNM) and the "r" prefix denoting recurrence (rTNM) [2].
  • Tumour grade is reported using the Nottingham Histologic Score (grades 1–3, derived from tubule formation, nuclear pleomorphism, and mitotic count) [2].
  • The Nottingham Prognostic Index (NPI) is used post-surgery to estimate prognosis, calculated as NPI = (0.2 × tumour size in cm) + lymph node stage + tumour grade, stratifying patients into four groups: I (excellent, ≤2.4), II (good, >2.4–≤3.4), III (moderate, >3.4–≤5.4), and IV (poor, >5.4) [1].
  • Response to neoadjuvant systemic therapy is reported using RECIST criteria: complete response (lesion not detectable), partial response (≥30% reduction in maximal diameter), stable disease (<30% reduction), and progressive disease (≥20% increase) [1].
  • Phyllodes tumours (a mixed connective/epithelial tumour distinct from epithelial breast cancer) are classified into benign, borderline, or malignant based on stromal atypia, mitotic activity, and margin infiltration [2].

Treatment and Management

  • Management is multidisciplinary, combining surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy (chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and/or hormonal therapy), with treatment sequencing guided by tumour stage and biology [1][2].
  • Neoadjuvant systemic therapy (chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or endocrine therapy before surgery) is indicated for locally advanced disease (T3–T4/N2–N3) to downsize the tumour, for HER2-positive tumours, for triple-negative breast cancer, and to facilitate breast-conserving surgery; neoadjuvant trastuzumab plus pertuzumab with chemotherapy is standard for HER2-positive tumours ≥2 cm or node-positive [1][2].
  • Adjuvant radiotherapy is indicated after breast-conserving surgery in essentially all patients, after mastectomy for tumours ≥5 cm, chest wall/skin involvement, lymphovascular invasion, grade 3 disease, or axillary node metastasis, and for locally advanced disease; post-mastectomy radiotherapy (PMRT) reduces 5-year isolated locoregional recurrence from 23% to 6% and 15-year breast cancer mortality from 60.1% to 54.7% in node-positive disease [1][2].
  • Adjuvant chemotherapy is indicated for invasive carcinomas >1 cm, smaller tumours with poor prognostic factors (lymphovascular invasion, high grade, HER2-positive, or triple-negative), and node-positive tumours, most commonly using anthracycline (doxorubicin/epirubicin) and taxane (paclitaxel/docetaxel)-based regimens; a meta-analysis found that chemotherapy regimens including a taxane or higher cumulative anthracycline dosing reduce breast cancer mortality by about one-third [1][2].
  • HER2-targeted therapy with trastuzumab (with or without pertuzumab) improves disease-free and overall survival in HER2-positive disease; adjuvant T-DM1 is indicated for residual disease after neoadjuvant HER2-targeted therapy [1][2].
  • Endocrine therapy with tamoxifen (a selective ER modulator) is used in premenopausal patients for 5–10 years, while aromatase inhibitors (anastrozole, letrozole, exemestane) are used in postmenopausal patients and show superior disease-free survival compared with tamoxifen alone [1][2].
  • Additional agents include CDK4/6 inhibitors (abemaciclib) added to endocrine therapy for high-risk hormone-receptor-positive disease, the PARP inhibitor olaparib for BRCA-mutated HER2-negative disease, and pembrolizumab for high-risk triple-negative breast cancer [2].
  • Chemoprevention with tamoxifen reduces invasive breast cancer risk by 43–50% in high-risk women, and women with a BRCA mutation may be offered bilateral risk-reducing mastectomy (reducing risk by about 90%) or chemoprophylaxis (reducing risk to about 50%) [1][2].
  • Metastatic (stage IV) disease is treated palliatively with endocrine therapy for hormone-receptor-positive disease with bone/limited visceral metastasis, chemotherapy for hormone-receptor-negative or visceral crisis, palliative radiotherapy for painful bony deposits, and bisphosphonates [1].
  • Follow-up after treatment includes clinical examination every 3 months for 2 years, then every 6 months for 3 years, then yearly, with annual mammography [1].

Surgeries

  • Surgical options for the breast primary in early-stage disease (0, I, II) are mastectomy or breast-conserving surgery (BCS); randomized trials (NSABP B-06, Milan I, and others) established no survival difference between mastectomy and BCS plus radiotherapy (breast-conserving therapy, BCT) [1][2].
  • Mastectomy is indicated for tumours large relative to breast size, multicentric disease, diffuse suspicious microcalcification, BRCA-positive cancers, contraindications to radiation, or patient preference [1][2].
  • The historical Halsted radical mastectomy (breast, skin, pectoralis major/minor, and axillary levels I–III en bloc) has been abandoned in favour of less morbid approaches after the NSABP B-04 trial showed no survival advantage over total mastectomy [1][2].
  • Simple/total mastectomy removes the breast, nipple-areolar complex, and axillary tail; modified radical mastectomy (MRM) adds level I–III axillary lymph node dissection; skin-sparing and nipple-sparing mastectomy preserve the skin envelope (and nipple-areolar complex, respectively) for immediate reconstruction [1][2].
  • BCS (lumpectomy, wide local excision) removes the tumour with a clear margin ("no ink on tumour" for invasive cancer; 2 mm for DCIS) while preserving the remainder of the breast; oncoplastic techniques (volume displacement or volume replacement with local/distant flaps) are used to optimise cosmesis, particularly when more than 20% of breast volume is excised [1][2].
  • Sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB), using blue dye and/or radiolabelled colloid (or indocyanine green), has replaced routine axillary lymph node dissection (ALND) for staging the clinically node-negative axilla, based on the NSABP B-32 trial; ALND is reserved for patients with clinically positive nodes, more than two to three positive sentinel nodes, or those not receiving whole breast irradiation (per ACOSOG Z0011 and AMAROS trial criteria) [1][2].
  • SLNB is contraindicated in inflammatory breast cancer and T4 disease, for which ALND should always be performed [1][2].
  • Locally advanced and inflammatory breast cancer is treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by modified radical mastectomy (without immediate reconstruction) and post-mastectomy radiotherapy [1][2].
  • Breast reconstruction can be immediate or delayed, using silicone implants or autologous tissue flaps (latissimus dorsi, TRAM, or DIEP free flap) [1].
  • Paget disease is treated by mastectomy with axillary staging or by wide local excision of the nipple-areolar complex (e.g. with a Grisotti flap) with adjuvant radiation [2].
  • In males, treatment mirrors that in females, with modified radical mastectomy (including a 2-cm margin and portion of pectoralis major if involved) plus radiotherapy and sentinel node biopsy for staging [1][2].

Complications

  • Axillary lymph node dissection is the main source of surgical morbidity in early-stage breast cancer, causing acute and chronic pain, paraesthesia, reduced shoulder range of motion, and lymphedema of the ipsilateral arm, described in some series to affect up to 80% of patients when combined with adjuvant radiation; sentinel node biopsy alone causes substantially less lymphedema than ALND [2].
  • The axilla should not be irradiated after axillary node dissection, as this further increases lymphedema risk [1].
  • Anthracycline chemotherapy carries a dose-dependent risk of cardiomyopathy/congestive heart failure (1.5–3% with modern regimens) and a small risk of secondary leukemia (<1%); taxanes are associated with peripheral neuropathy but not cardiac toxicity [2].
  • Trastuzumab-based regimens carry increased risk of cardiac dysfunction, particularly when combined with anthracyclines [2].
  • Tamoxifen carries risks of thromboembolic events, endometrial cancer, and cataracts; aromatase inhibitors are associated with bone density loss and fracture risk, requiring a baseline bone density scan [1][2].
  • Radiotherapy after silicone implant reconstruction often leads to a high incidence of capsular contracture and unacceptable cosmetic results [1].
  • Stewart-Treves syndrome is a secondary angiosarcoma developing in the chronically lymphedematous upper extremity after radical mastectomy and irradiation, historically 10–15 years later [2].
  • Local recurrence after lumpectomy without radiotherapy occurred in 39.2% of patients in the NSABP B-06 trial versus 14.3% with radiotherapy, underscoring recurrence as a key long-term complication of undertreatment [2].
  • Positive surgical margins are associated with a twofold increase in ipsilateral breast tumour recurrence risk compared with negative margins [2].

Prognosis

  • Five-year survival in the United States has improved from 63% in the early 1960s to 91% during 2012–2018, attributed to earlier detection via screening and improved therapy [2].
  • Key disease-related prognostic factors include tumour size, stage at diagnosis, axillary lymph node involvement, tumour grade, histopathological subtype (metaplastic carcinoma is particularly aggressive), HER2-positive and triple-negative receptor status, presence of lymphovascular invasion, extensive DCIS component, and high Ki-67 index; patient-related adverse factors include younger age, premenopausal status, BRCA-associated tumours, family history, prior breast cancer, obesity/sedentary lifestyle, and failure to complete intended treatment [1].
  • The Nottingham Prognostic Index (combining tumour size, nodal stage, and grade) stratifies patients into four prognostic groups from excellent to poor [1].
  • DCIS treated with breast-conserving therapy or mastectomy carries a survival rate of 98–99% with either approach, although local recurrence rates differ (1–2% per year with BCT versus 1–2% lifetime with mastectomy) [2].
  • Pathologic complete response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy is associated with improved event-free and overall survival, most strongly in triple-negative and HER2-positive, hormone-receptor-negative subtypes [2].
  • Survival in males with breast cancer is similar to that in females matched for age and stage, although males are typically diagnosed at a more advanced stage due to lower awareness [2].
  • Angiosarcoma of the breast, whether primary or radiation-associated, is the most aggressive breast tumour and carries a very poor prognosis, with reported outcomes ranging from 28% to 82% 10-year survival depending on grade and size [1][2].
  • Locally advanced breast cancer treated with surgery alone (before 1970s multimodal treatment) had local relapse rates of 30–50% and mortality of 70%; with modern trimodal treatment (neoadjuvant chemotherapy, mastectomy, and radiotherapy), locoregional recurrence for inflammatory breast cancer has been reduced to as low as 6.9%, though distant recurrence remains high at 35.1% at 5 years [2].
