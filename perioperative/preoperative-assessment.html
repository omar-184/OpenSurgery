Preoperative Assessment

Summary

  • Preoperative assessment is the systematic process of history-taking, examination, investigation and risk stratification carried out before surgery to identify comorbidity, optimise the patient's condition, and match the planned operation and anaesthetic to the patient's physiological reserve [1].
  • The stress of major surgery can increase oxygen demand by up to 40%, with inflammatory, endocrine, hypercoagulable and fluid-shift changes persisting for several postoperative days.
  • Careful preoperative planning aims to minimise these unwanted effects [1].
  • A structured, systems-based evaluation (covering surgical objectives, urgency, risk assessment and informed consent) is now considered central to modern perioperative surgical practice [2].

Definition

Preoperative assessment is defined by its purpose: systematic history, examination and investigation to assess functional reserve and to formulate advice on optimisation so the patient can best cope with anticipated operative stress, ideally through a multidisciplinary team including the primary care physician, specialist nurses, physiotherapist, dietician and perioperative physician [1]. It encompasses determination of surgical objectives (disease prevention, disease control, or symptom palliation), classification of urgency (elective, urgent, or emergent), and risk assessment prior to obtaining informed consent [2].

Physiological demand of surgery

  • Surgery and anaesthesia impose a physiological demand for increased oxygen delivery: postoperative oxygen demand rises from an average of 110 mL/min/m² at rest to 170 mL/min/m² [1].
  • Most patients meet this by increasing cardiac output and tissue oxygen extraction, but patients with limited cardiorespiratory reserve are at risk of oxygen debt.
  • Occult hypovolaemia from fluid shift or blood loss can further impair oxygen delivery, and splanchnic vasoconstriction to compensate for this may cause gut ischaemia, while patients with coronary or cerebrovascular disease face a higher risk of myocardial ischaemia or stroke [1].
  • Tissue destruction, blood loss, fluid shifts, temperature change, pain and anxiety all contribute to this demand [1].

Assessment components

  • Preoperative assessment is organised around a thorough past medical, surgical and anaesthetic history (including recreational drug/alcohol use, drug history and allergies), examination, and targeted investigation [1].
  • Key domains assessed on history include cardiovascular disease (valvular disease, ischaemic heart disease, hypertension, heart failure, dysrhythmia, peripheral vascular disease, cardiac devices), respiratory disease (COPD, asthma, infections, obstructive sleep apnoea), gastrointestinal/liver disease, genitourinary/renal disease, neurological and psychiatric disease, endocrine/metabolic disease (diabetes, thyroid, phaeochromocytoma), locomotor disease, haematological disease (bleeding disorders, thrombosis history), active infection, and previous surgical/anaesthetic problems such as a difficult airway or suxamethonium apnoea [1].
  • Examination should be at minimum cardiorespiratory with airway assessment, looking for signs of heart failure, valvular disease, peripheral vascular disease and respiratory disease [1].
  • Airway assessment uses the modified Mallampati class, mouth opening >3 cm, thyromental distance >6.5 cm, thyrosternal distance >12.5 cm, jaw protrusion and atlanto-occipital extension [1].
  • In the geriatric population, additional clinical domains include cognitive impairment/delirium risk, depression, medication management/polypharmacy, and functional status and frailty [2].

The ACS NSQIP/AGS geriatric assessment in Schwartz's account

  • Assessment aims to minimise risk rather than "clear" the patient and begins at the decision for surgery; function is graded by activities of daily living (dressing, bathing, toileting, transferring, continence, eating) and instrumental activities (shopping, laundry, transport, finances, medications, cooking, housekeeping), with vision, hearing, swallowing, falls in the past year and gait assessed by the Timed Up and Go (stand from a chair, walk 10 feet, turn, return and sit) where 15 seconds or more means high fall risk and referral to physiotherapy, and in surgical patients over 65 "slow" (≥15 s) versus "fast" (≤10 s) times carried 52–77% versus 11–13% complications and 31% versus 3% one-year mortality [4].
  • Frailty is measured by Fried's criteria or Robinson's clinical alternatives, Mini-Cog ≤3, albumin ≤3 g/dL, a fall in 6 months and haematocrit under 35%, or functional impairment (TUG ≥15 s, ADL dependence) with a Charlson index ≥3 [4].
  • Cognition is screened in everyone over 65 with the Mini-Cog (three-word recall (banana, sunrise, chair) plus a clock drawn to 11:10; 0–2 suggests impairment, 3–5 none), corroborated by someone who knows the patient and referred if declining; decision-making capacity requires that the patient state a choice, understand the information, acknowledge condition, options and outcomes, and reason about them; depression (up to 11% over 71; risks female sex, disability, bereavement, sleep disturbance, poor health, isolation) is screened with the PHQ-2 (two weeks of low mood or anhedonia in the past year); alcohol (13% of men and 8% of women over 65 take two or more drinks daily, 14.5% and 3.3% five or more) is screened with the modified CAGE and drinkers receive multivitamins with folate and 100 mg thiamine [4].
  • Cardiac evaluation follows the ACC/AHA steps (emergency; active conditions to cardiology; NSQIP calculator for major cardiac event risk; under 1% needs no testing; otherwise functional capacity, then pharmacological stress testing if poor and only if it will change care), no routine ECG for low-risk surgery; cardiac output falls about 1% a year, heart rate augmentation blunts and output depends on preload-driven dilatation so volume status is critical, impaired cardiac function under stress causes over half of postoperative deaths and 11% of complications in the elderly, and heart failure (10% over 65) is the leading cause of postoperative morbidity and mortality [4].
  • Pulmonary complications (atelectasis, pneumonia, prolonged ventilation) affect up to 15% over 70; patient risks are age over 60, COPD, ASA II or higher, dependence, heart failure, sleep apnoea, pulmonary hypertension, smoking, impaired sensorium, sepsis, weight loss over 10% in 6 months, albumin under 3.5 and urea ≥7.5 mmol/L, procedural risks operations over 3 hours, site, emergency, general anaesthesia, transfusion and residual block (obesity, controlled asthma and diabetes are not risks) and optimisation includes smoking cessation, inspiratory muscle training and selective radiography and spirometry, routine batteries being discouraged [4].
  • Nutrition screening documents BMI (under 18.5 refers), albumin and prealbumin (albumin under 3.0 g/dL without liver or kidney disease refers) and unintentional loss over 10–15% in 6 months; ASPEN counters that albumin reflects inflammation and defines malnutrition by any of intake under 75% of need for a month, weight loss over 5% in a month, 7.5% in 3, 10% in 6 or 20% in a year, muscle wasting (temples, clavicles, shoulders, interossei, scapula, thigh, calf), subcutaneous fat loss (orbital, triceps, ribs), fluid accumulation masking loss, and reduced grip strength [4].
  • Medication review covers over-the-counter, NSAID, vitamin, eye-drop, topical and herbal products (herbals stopped 7 days before), applies the Beers criteria, continues drugs with withdrawal potential (SSRIs, tricyclics, benzodiazepines, antipsychotics, MAOIs, β-blockers, clonidine, statins, corticosteroids) and ACE inhibitors or ARBs unless hypertension is well controlled, reduces benzodiazepines, avoids pethidine and diphenhydramine-type antihistamines while neuroleptics and digoxin carry no delirium excess, continues β-blockers (started for independent indications or intermediate-risk or vascular surgery with multiple risk factors) and starts statins early for vascular disease, high LDL or thallium ischaemia, doses by GFR rather than creatinine since the GFR:creatinine ratio falls with age, and watches polypharmacy, 40% over 65 take five or more drugs from more than one doctor, 68% add over-the-counter products, and adverse reactions, cognitive impairment and mortality rise with the count [4].
  • Counselling confirms an advance directive and health-care proxy, documents goals and expectations, describes the postoperative course including functional decline and rehabilitation or nursing home care, and assesses social support, because surrogates agreed with patients only 59–88% of the time despite 90% of patients expecting accuracy, and among elders with limited life expectancy 99% would accept a low-burden treatment to restore health but 74% and 89% would refuse it if it left severe functional or cognitive impairment; the immediate checklist confirms goals, directives and proxy, discusses new risks against existing directives, allows clear fluids to 2 hours, adheres to antibiotic and VTE prophylaxis and ensures essential drugs are taken and non-essential stopped, with palliative consultation for those with under 6 months to live [4].

Sources of risk

  • Risk of perioperative morbidity and mortality arises from an interaction of patient factors and surgery-specific factors [1].
  • Patient factors that predispose to high risk include previous severe cardiorespiratory illness (MI, COPD, stroke), late-stage aortic vascular disease, age over 70 with limited organ reserve, extensive cancer surgery, acute abdominal catastrophe with haemodynamic instability, massive blood loss (>8 units), septicaemia, respiratory failure and acute renal failure [1].
  • Surgery-specific risk is stratified into high (>5% cardiac risk: open aortic, major vascular, urgent body-cavity, major neurosurgery), intermediate (1–5%: elective abdominal, carotid, endovascular aneurysm repair) and low risk (<1%: breast, dental, thyroid, ophthalmic) procedures [1].
  • The elderly are not independently at higher risk but have more cardiac, pulmonary and renal disease and require surgery four times as often as the rest of the population.
  • Around 10% of over-65s are frail [1].

Frailty, falls and delirium in the older surgical patient in Schwartz's account

  • Fried's phenotype defines frailty from five criteria, unintentional weight loss of 10 lb or 5% in a year, exhaustion on two CES-D items, low activity (under 383 kcal a week in men, 270 in women), slow 15-foot walk time (7 seconds or more, or 6 for the taller) and weak grip stratified by sex and body mass index, one or two criteria being "prefrail" and three or more "frail"; frailty overlaps but is distinct from comorbidity and disability, predicts postoperative adverse events, longer stay and discharge to a facility, and Rockwood's deficit-accumulation index (92 symptoms, signs, impairments and laboratory values from the Canadian Study of Health and Aging, accumulating at about 3% a year) suits registries such as NSQIP more than the clinic; other geriatric syndromes (incontinence, falls, pressure ulcers, delirium, functional decline) share risk factors with it [4].
  • One in three adults over 65 falls each year, nearly 60% fall again within a year, half cannot get up (a "long lie" causing lasting decline), the Joint Commission made fall prevention a 2015 safety goal, and among 7982 elective surgical patients one, two or three or more preoperative falls predicted postoperative falls at 30 days (adjusted OR 2.3, 3.6, 5.5) and a year (2.3, 3.4, 6.9), functional decline and in-hospital complications [4].
  • Delirium (an acute fluctuating disorder of attention and awareness (DSM-5)) affects up to a third of inpatients over 70 (half present on admission), ranges from 4–5% after cataract or urological surgery to 50–60% after infrarenal aneurysm repair or hip fracture, and doubles or triples one-year mortality; preoperative risks are age 70 or more, cognitive impairment, poor function, alcohol abuse, abnormal sodium, potassium or glucose, and intrathoracic or aortic surgery, intraoperative blood loss with a haematocrit under 30% adds risk, undertreated pain is a major postoperative trigger, anticholinergics (tricyclics, antihistamines, H2-blockers, antimuscarinics, promethazine, olanzapine, paroxetine), corticosteroids, pethidine and sedative-hypnotics should be avoided, and bundles of sensory enhancement (glasses, hearing aids), early mobility, orientation, therapeutic activity and sleep protocols reduce it; established delirium is worked up for drugs, electrolytes, withdrawal, infection, restraints, sensory deprivation, intracranial events, retention or impaction and metabolic causes (including PE, MI, uraemia, ammonia, thyroid), screened with the Nu-DESC, and treated pharmacologically only for danger to self or others, haloperidol 0.5 mg orally (1–2 hours) or IM/IV (20–40 minutes), most responding to 1–2 mg total, withheld if QTc exceeds 500, with benzodiazepines only for withdrawal [4].

Investigations

  • National guidance sets out the investigations needed according to the category of elective surgery and the ASA grade, and the UK version of that grid is set out below [1].
  • Commonly used preoperative tests include full blood count (major operations, elderly, anaemia), HbA1c (diabetics not tested in the last 3 months), sickle cell test (if indicated by history), urea and electrolytes (major operations, age >65, cardiovascular/renal/endocrine disease), liver function tests (jaundice, known/suspected hepatitis, malignancy, alcohol excess), clotting screen (bleeding diathesis, liver disease, anticoagulant use), ECG (age >65 or symptomatic with relevant history), chest radiograph (only if clinically indicated), echocardiogram (symptomatic murmurs or heart failure signs), pregnancy testing, and other tests including venous bicarbonate/sleep studies for suspected obstructive sleep apnoea, arterial blood gases, group and save/cross-match, MRSA and cardiopulmonary exercise testing for high-risk surgery [1].
  • Airway difficulty prediction uses the modified Mallampati classification (Grade 1: fauces, pillars, soft palate and uvula seen; Grade 4: hard palate only seen) [1].
  • Functional physical fitness can be estimated using metabolic equivalent tasks (METs; 1 MET = 3.5 mL O2/kg/min).
  • Patients able to achieve >4 METs (e.g. climbing one flight of stairs) are generally accepted for low-risk surgery, though this subjective assessment may be overestimated by patients.
  • The Duke Activity Status Index (DASI) is a less subjective alternative [1].
  • Cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) is the gold-standard objective fitness measurement for high-risk surgery, identifying the anaerobic threshold (AT) and peak oxygen consumption (VO2 peak).
  • An AT below 11 mL/kg/min or VO2 peak below 15 mL/kg/min predicts higher morbidity/mortality after major surgery.
  • Where CPET is unavailable, the incremental shuttle walk test (ISWT) is a lower-cost alternative.
  • Failure to achieve 350 metres predicts higher risk for oesophageal surgery [1].
  • In geriatric patients, cognitive assessment can use the Mini-Cog (three-item recall and clock draw, taking about 3 minutes), and depression screening uses the Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2) [2].
  • Frailty can be measured using the Timed Up & Go (TUG) test, timing a patient rising from a chair, walking 3 metres, turning and returning to sit.
  • TUG >20 seconds is associated with a 50% risk of major complications in patients over 70 undergoing elective solid-malignancy surgery, versus 14% with TUG ≤20 seconds [2].

Scoring and Severity

  • The American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification is the most widely used categorical risk tool: ASA I (normal healthy patient), II (mild systemic disease, no functional limitation), III (severe systemic disease, definite functional limitation), IV (severe systemic disease, constant threat to life), V (moribund, unlikely to survive 24 hours with or without operation), VI (declared brain-dead organ donor), with "E" appended for emergency operations; 30-day mortality rises from 0.1% (ASA I) to 93.3% (ASA V) [1].
  • The ASA classification was not designed as a risk-prediction tool and does not account for age or type of surgery, and the term "systemic disease" introduces subjectivity [1].
  • Lee's Revised Cardiac Risk Index (RCRI) uses ischaemic heart disease, heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, renal insufficiency (creatinine >177 µmol/L) and high-risk surgery as weighted risk factors, stratifying risk of major cardiac complications from 0.4% (0 factors) to 11.0% (≥3 factors) [1].
  • Other scoring systems include POSSUM/P-POSSUM (Physiologic and Operative Severity Score for the enUmeration of Mortality and morbidity), the ACS NSQIP surgical risk score/calculator (estimates complication/death risk for >1000 procedures using 19–20 patient-specific preoperative factors and CPT-coded procedure risk, publicly available online, with c-statistics >0.75 for most outcomes and >0.9 for 30-day mortality), APACHE-II, Glasgow aneurysm score, Hardman index, Surgical Outcome Risk Tool (SORT), Boey score, Mannheim peritonitis index and the NELA score [1][2].
  • The Royal College of Surgeons of England recommends active consultant input for patients with predicted mortality >5%, and direct consultant supervision plus postoperative critical care for those with predicted mortality >10% [1].
  • The European Society of Cardiology/Anaesthesiology stratifies surgery into low (<1%), intermediate (1–5%) and high (>5%) 30-day cardiac event risk categories [2].
  • Additional general risk-scoring systems referenced across the surgical literature include EuroSCORE (cardiac surgery) and the Glasgow/Ranson criteria for pancreatitis-specific mortality prediction [5].
  • Child's classification (Child–Turcotte) grades hepatic dysfunction risk as A (minimal), B (moderate) or C (advanced/high mortality) using serum bilirubin, albumin, ascites, encephalopathy and nutrition [5].

Symptom-based functional grading: NYHA and MRC

  • Two symptom-graded scales sit alongside the categorical risk tools and are the ones actually recorded in a clerking.
  • The New York Heart Association functional classification grades cardiac disability in four steps: class 1, cardiac disease present but no symptoms and no limitation of ordinary physical activity such as walking and climbing stairs; class 2, mild symptoms (mild shortness of breath and/or angina) with slight limitation during ordinary activity; class 3, marked limitation caused by symptoms even during less-than-ordinary activity such as walking 20 to 100 metres, comfortable only at rest; and class 4, severe limitation with symptoms even at rest, mostly bedbound [6].
  • A higher NYHA class is associated with a worse prognosis, and perioperative mortality increases with higher NYHA class and with preoperative pulmonary congestion [6][7].
  • Decompensated heart failure may be aggravated by the fluid shifts of anaesthesia and abdominal surgery, so it should be treated to lower filling pressures and improve cardiac output before elective surgery, with the patient stable for a week before operation [7].
  • The respiratory equivalent is the MRC dyspnoea scale: 1, not troubled by breathlessness except on strenuous exercise; 2, short of breath when hurrying or walking up a slight hill; 3, walks slower than contemporaries on level ground because of breathlessness, or has to stop for breath when walking at own pace; 4, stops for breath after about 100 metres or after a few minutes on level ground; 5, too breathless to leave the house, or breathless when dressing [6].

Treatment and Management

  • Common comorbidities are optimised to the best possible level before elective surgery.
  • Cardiovascular: patients on beta-blockers/statins should continue these perioperatively; ACE inhibitors/receptor blockers are often omitted 24 hours before surgery to prevent intraoperative hypotension; elective surgery is postponed 3–6 months after myocardial infarction; blood pressure should be controlled to <160/100 mmHg preoperatively (Bailey & Love) or delayed if >180 mmHg (Oxford Handbook) prior to elective surgery; warfarin is stopped 5 days preoperatively for an INR ≤1.5, and direct oral anticoagulants generally stopped 2–3 days preoperatively with longer intervals in renal impairment [1][8].
  • Respiratory: patients continue regular inhalers until anaesthesia induction, smoking cessation is encouraged (ideally ≥6 weeks preoperatively, minimum 7 days), and preoperative inspiratory muscle training and chest physiotherapy reduce pulmonary complications; regional techniques are considered in severe disease [1][9].
  • Gastrointestinal: standard fasting is 6 hours for solids/non-clear fluids, 2 hours for clear fluids, and 4 hours for breast milk in infants [1].
  • Renal: chronic renal failure patients should avoid hypovolaemia/nephrotoxic drugs, and dialysis-dependent patients are dialysed the day before surgery with FBC/U&Es checked pre- and post-dialysis [1][5].
  • Hepatic: elective surgery is postponed until acute decompensation resolves; vitamin K may be given for coagulopathy of cholestatic jaundice [5].
  • Endocrine: HbA1c <69 mmol/mol is recommended before elective surgery; diabetic patients are placed first on operating lists, and variable-rate intravenous insulin infusion is used for insulin-dependent patients undergoing major surgery [1]; a stepwise regimen for minor/major/emergency surgery is detailed with specific sliding-scale insulin doses by blood sugar range [5].
  • Steroid-dependent patients (>5 mg prednisolone for >2 weeks, or reduced within 2–4 weeks, or post-adrenalectomy) require perioperative hydrocortisone cover (25–100 mg four times daily, or infusion) to prevent Addisonian crisis [5].
  • Obesity: morbidly obese patients (BMI >35) should be counselled on weight loss/exercise, with prophylaxis for aspiration and DVT if surgery cannot be delayed; severe obstructive sleep apnoea requires 6 weeks of preoperative nocturnal CPAP [1].
  • Thromboprophylaxis: DVT risk assessment is performed on all patients preoperatively, and oestrogen-containing contraceptives/HRT should be considered for discontinuation 4 weeks before surgery [1].
  • High-risk patients (predicted mortality ≥5%) should be admitted to critical care where indicated [1].
  • Informed consent, covering diagnosis, proposed procedure, procedure-related risks, likelihood of success, mental capacity and alternatives, is a mandatory, integral part of preoperative management [2].
  • For emergency surgery, the same principles of assessment apply but optimisation is time-limited.
  • Urgency is graded (e.g.
  • NCEPOD classification: immediate, urgent, expedited, elective) and medical treatment should be started even if it cannot be completed before a time-critical operation [1].
NICE NG45 · NICE NG180

NG45 is the guideline that abolished the routine preoperative panel in the UK, and it is best learned as a grid of three surgery grades against three ASA bands. The tests it covers are chest X-ray, resting echocardiography, resting ECG, full blood count, HbA1c, haemostasis tests, kidney function, lung function and arterial blood gases, polysomnography, pregnancy testing, sickle cell tests and urine tests, developed against five comorbidities: cardiovascular, diabetes, obesity, renal and respiratory [10].

Surgery grade is defined by worked examples rather than by definition: minor (excising a skin lesion, draining a breast abscess), intermediate (primary inguinal hernia repair, varicose vein excision, tonsillectomy, knee arthroscopy), and major or complex (total abdominal hysterectomy, endoscopic resection of prostate, lumbar discectomy, thyroidectomy, total joint replacement, lung operations) [10]. ASA is quoted verbatim, with a UK gloss NICE flags as an interpretation rather than an endorsement: anaesthetists in the UK often qualify these grades as relating to functional capacity, that is comorbidity that does not (ASA 2) or does (ASA 3) limit a person's activity [10].

  • Four tests are named as things not to do routinely, and they are the ones historically ordered by habit. Do not routinely offer chest X-rays before surgery [10]. Do not routinely offer resting echocardiography
  • Consider it only if the person has a heart murmur and any cardiac symptom (breathlessness, pre-syncope, syncope or chest pain), or signs or symptoms of heart failure, and carry out a resting ECG and discuss the findings with an anaesthetist before ordering it [10]. Do not routinely offer urine dipstick tests
  • Consider microscopy and culture of a midstream sample only if the presence of a urinary tract infection would influence the decision to operate [10].
  • And do not routinely offer testing for sickle cell disease or trait
  • Ask instead whether the person or any family member has sickle cell disease, and liaise with the specialist service if they do [10].

HbA1c splits by whether diabetes is already diagnosed. Do not routinely offer HbA1c testing to people without diagnosed diabetes [10]. For people with diabetes, the most recent HbA1c should be included in the referral from primary care, and HbA1c testing is offered if they have not been tested in the last 3 months [10].

Pregnancy testing is a documentation requirement as much as a test. On the day of surgery, sensitively ask all women of childbearing potential whether there is any possibility they could be pregnant; make sure those who could be are aware of the risks of the anaesthetic and procedure to the fetus; document all discussions about whether or not to carry out a test; and carry one out with consent if there is any doubt, under locally agreed protocols that are documented and audited [10].

  • NG180 supplies the framing that NG45 does not: risk, lifestyle and the enhanced recovery programme. Use a validated risk stratification tool to supplement clinical assessment when planning surgery, including dental surgery, and discuss the person's risks and surgical options with them for informed shared decision making [11]. Discuss lifestyle modifications, for example stopping smoking and reducing alcohol consumption [11]. Offer an enhanced recovery programme to people having elective major or complex surgery, and use one that includes preoperative, intraoperative and postoperative components [11].
  • When booking surgery, give people a point of contact within the perioperative care team for information and support before and after their operation [11].
  • On preoperative optimisation clinics for older people, NICE is candid: there was not enough clear evidence to show whether the benefits outweigh the costs, so it made a recommendation for research instead [11].

Optimisation, prehabilitation and outcomes in older adults in Schwartz's account

  • Fasting beyond 6 hours has no clear benefit; the 2011 ASA guidance stops clear fluids at 2 hours, light food and non-human milk at 6 and fried or fatty food and meat at 8, longer for diabetes or hiatus hernia; appropriate prophylactic antibiotics within 60 minutes of incision give older adults a 60-day mortality benefit; VTE prophylaxis is LMWH from 13 hours before or 12 hours after arthroplasty or hip fracture surgery for 10–14 up to 35 days, LMWH or low-dose unfractionated heparin plus intermittent pneumatic compression for general, abdominopelvic, bariatric, vascular, plastic and thoracic surgery, compression alone for craniotomy, spinal and cardiac surgery, and heparin plus compression after major trauma or cord injury [4].
  • Prehabilitation works: the NHS Proactive Care of Older People undergoing Surgery (POPS) geriatric team, daily rounds, weekly multidisciplinary meetings, consultant or nurse-specialist ward rounds, cut pneumonia from 20% to 4%, delirium from 19% to 4%, poor pain control from 30% to 2%, delayed mobilisation from 28% to 9% and inappropriate catheters from 37% to 7%, and stay by 4.5 days; the Michigan Surgical Home and Optimization Program's home walking with reminders, incentive spirometry from a week before, nutrition, stress and care-planning education and smoking cessation engaged 82% and cut stay 31% and cost 28% [4].
  • Surgical palliative care competencies span guiding the transition from curative to palliative goals, symptom assessment and management, palliative procedures, breaking bad news, family meetings on directives, withdrawal and withholding of support, pain and non-pain symptom knowledge, ethics and law, grief, prognostication, spirituality, communication, professionalism and systems-based referral to hospice and pastoral or social services; a decision aid presenting prognosis increased choice of less aggressive options in frail (OR 3.41) and demented (OR 1.66) patients, preoperative palliative consultation reduced mortality in frail elders and postoperative palliative care improves symptoms and quality of life, and palliative surgery (least-invasive relief of vomiting or pain without new symptoms) must be presented realistically because after surgery for malignant bowel obstruction from carcinomatosis many suffer complications, incomplete relief and hospitalisation disproportionate to remaining life [4].

Influence on operative choice

Preoperative assessment is a diagnostic and optimisation process rather than an operative procedure. The source texts describe how assessment findings influence choice of operation (e.g. minimally invasive versus open surgery, laparoscopic surgery favoured in patients at risk of postoperative respiratory complications) rather than describing preoperative assessment itself as a surgical procedure [1].

Consequences of inadequate assessment

  • Failure to adequately assess and plan can have fatal consequences, particularly with airway management [1].
  • Perioperative myocardial infarction carries a mortality of 15–25% [1].
  • Postoperative respiratory failure (PaO2 <8 kPa in air, or inability to extubate 48 hours after surgery) is associated with 27–40% mortality, and 1.5% of patients develop lower respiratory tract infection postoperatively with >20% 30-day mortality [1].
  • High-risk surgical patients account for less than 15% of all procedures but contribute to more than 80% of perioperative deaths in the UK, yet only 15–30% of high-risk patients are admitted to critical care after surgery [1].
  • In the geriatric population, unrecognised cognitive impairment strongly predicts postoperative delirium, which occurs in nearly 50% of geriatric patients and has a major impact on length of stay, long-term cognition, cost of care and mortality [2].
  • Risk factors for postoperative delirium include age >65, cognitive impairment, severe comorbidity, hearing/vision impairment, hip fracture, infection, inadequate pain control, depression, alcohol use, sleep disturbance, renal insufficiency, anaemia, hypoxia/hypercarbia, poor nutrition, dehydration, electrolyte abnormalities, poor functional status, immobilisation, polypharmacy (especially benzodiazepines, anticholinergics, antihistamines, antipsychotics), urinary retention/catheter, and aortic procedures [2].
  • Unrecognised obstructive sleep apnoea is associated with a higher incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) postoperatively [1].

Outcomes in the elderly in Schwartz's figures

  • Adults over 60 take months to regain basic and up to 6 months instrumental activities after abdominal surgery and are often discharged to facilities even after uncomplicated courses; most nursing home residents never regain baseline function, after colectomy for cancer 53% are dead at a year and over half of survivors decline, after lower-limb bypass half die within a year with only 13% ambulatory and 18% at or above baseline, and after hip fracture over a third die and over half die or decline within 180 days, worst with comorbidity, advanced dementia or no fixation [4].
  • Half of cancers are diagnosed at 70 or older and oncological operations in the elderly were forecast to rise 50% by 2020 without trial data, registries showing excess complications and death especially in liver and rectal surgery, so surgery must be justified by safe cure without loss of function or quality of life; emergency surgery is exceptionally dangerous, 54% 90-day mortality after emergency gastrointestinal operations over 90, 35% 30-day mortality after endovascular repair of ruptured aneurysm and 52% after open conversion, two- to threefold higher operative mortality among 70,000 nursing home residents having emergency abdominal surgery than matched community elders with far more life-sustaining interventions (18% vs 5% after cholecystectomy, 55% vs 43% after ulcer surgery), and in appendicitis and cholecystitis a third of elders lack leukocytosis, a third fever and a third localised peritonitis, tripling perforation and gangrene, so an "unimpressive" examination never excludes surgical disease [4].
  • In cardiac surgery age is not the strongest predictor: emergency, NYHA class 3 or more and renal failure are, non-dialysis renal dysfunction carrying 60% five-year mortality against 25%, cerebrovascular disease doubling death, octogenarians achieving 70.5% four-year survival; CABG mortality rises from 1.6% at 51–60 to 7.7% at 81–90 (7–12% overall including emergencies, about 2.8% electively), aortic valve replacement carries 3–10% (mean 7.7%) against 1.5–2 years' survival with untreated symptomatic stenosis so minimally symptomatic octogenarians should be treated early, mitral surgery up to 20%, about 30% over 70 suffer neurological dysfunction after valve surgery, bioprostheses are preferred to avoid anticoagulation in fall-prone patients (durable at 10 years), only critically stenosed vessels are bypassed in combined procedures, and TAVI matches or betters surgical replacement even at low-to-intermediate risk with acceptable long-term results; aneurysm prevalence rises from about 1% at 55–60 to 10% at 80, EVAR through femoral access under epidural cuts operative time, blood loss, ICU and stay for elders once unfit for open repair, and non-operative management is right when operative risk outweighs rupture risk or life expectancy is too short to benefit [4].

Outcomes

  • Perioperative mortality has declined substantially over recent decades: from 10,603 per million operations in the 1970s to 1,176 per million in the 1990s–2000s according to a systematic review by Bainbridge et al.

  • Increasing ASA class is associated with increased early postoperative mortality in both emergent and elective operations, with ASA class VE (moribund, emergency) associated with nearly 20% likelihood of early postoperative mortality [2].
  • For very low-risk outpatient procedures the risk of death is less than 1 in 50,000, whereas high-risk operations in critically ill patients can have expected mortality rates routinely exceeding 20% [2].
  • Preoperative frailty (e.g. by TUG test or ACS NSQIP frailty index) is a stronger predictor of postoperative cardiac arrest and death than ASA class or history of MI in some large studies [2].
  • Use of formal preoperative evaluation is associated with identification of patients at elevated respiratory risk, a 55% decrease in preoperative testing, an 88% reduction in case cancellations, reduced length of stay, cost reduction, and lower in-hospital mortality [12].
