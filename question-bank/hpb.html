Gallstone Disease and Cholecystitis

  • Q: Acute cholecystitis is considered: A. A primary infectious process with secondary inflammation. B. A sterile primary inflammatory process. C. A primary inflammatory process with occasional bacterial contamination. D. A primary autoimmune process. (Answer: C — Acute cholecystitis is initially an inflammatory process, probably mediated by the mucosal toxin lysolecithin, with secondary bacterial contamination documented in only 15% to 30% of patients.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 32)
  • Q: Over a 10-year period, what percentage of patients with asymptomatic gallstones will remain symptom-free? A. 10% B. 25% C. 50% D. 66% E. 90% (Answer: D — About two-thirds of asymptomatic patients with gallstones remain symptom-free, as only approximately 3% of asymptomatic individuals develop biliary colic per year.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 32)
  • Q: What constitutes as normal filling times in a fasting subject when utilizing a hepatobiliary iminodiacetic acid (HIDA) scan? A. Liver uptake within 15 minutes, and gallbladder, bile duct, and duodenum uptake within 60 minutes B. Liver uptake within 15 minutes, and gallbladder, bile duct, and duodenum uptake within 45 minutes C. Liver uptake within 10 minutes, and gallbladder, bile duct, and duodenum uptake within 45 minutes D. Liver uptake within 10 minutes, and gallbladder, bile duct, and duodenum uptake within 60 minutes (Answer: D — Liver uptake is usually detected within 10 minutes, and the gallbladder, bile ducts, and duodenum are typically visualized within 60 minutes in fasting subjects.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 32)
  • Q: A 36-year-old woman presents with several days of right upper quadrant (RUQ) abdominal pain; which of the following findings would best support a diagnosis acute cholecystitis? A. Cholelithiasis on ultrasound with a 2 mm gallbladder wall B. Delayed gallbladder filling on hepatobiliary iminodiacetic acid (HIDA) C. Lack of gallbladder filling on HIDA D. Decreased ejection fraction (EF) on HIDA (Answer: C — Lack of gallbladder filling on HIDA reflects inflammatory cystic duct obstruction and is highly diagnostic of acute cholecystitis, with sensitivity and specificity of about 95% each.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 32)
  • Q: A 26-year-old who is 25 weeks pregnant presents to the emergency department (ED) with several days of right upper quadrant (RUQ) associated with meals and ultrasound (US) concerning for acute cholecystitis, what is the best plan to proceed? A. Observation with antibiotics, elective cholecystectomy after delivery B. Antibiotics and supportive care C. Antibiotics and laparoscopic cholecystectomy this admission D. Antibiotics and laparoscopic cholecystectomy after 28 weeks of pregnancy (Answer: C — Pregnant women with symptomatic gallstones who cannot be managed expectantly can safely undergo laparoscopic cholecystectomy, ideally during the second trimester.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 32)
  • Q: A 76-year-old man who is medically unfit for surgery presents to the emergency department (ED) with right upper quadrant (RUQ) tenderness and ultrasound (US) findings concerning for acute cholecystitis, what is the best course of action? A. Antibiotics, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP), lap chole when stable this admission B. Antibiotics, lap chole when stable this admission C. Antibiotics, cholecystostomy tube, lap chole this admission D. Antibiotics, cholecystostomy tube, lap chole in 6 to 8 weeks if able (Answer: D — Patients medically unfit for surgery are treated with antibiotics and cholecystostomy tube decompression, with elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy scheduled within approximately 6 to 8 weeks if fitness recovers.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 32)
  • Q: What is the treatment for a gallstone ileus? A. Endoscopic retrieval at the ileocecal valve B. Surgical enterolithotomy to retrieve the stone and primary closure or bowel resection C. Surgical enterolithotomy to retrieve the stone if beyond the ileocecal valve D. Supportive management (Answer: B — Gallstone ileus is managed by surgical enterolithotomy, removing the stone through an enterotomy that is then repaired or resected depending on its size.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 32)
  • Q: Which of the following features is an indication for cholecystectomy? A. Gallbladder polyp >5 mm B. Gallbladder polyp >10 mm C. Three gallbladder polyps of various sizes D. An 8 mm polyp stable in size over 1 year (Answer: B — Gallbladder polyps measuring >10 mm carry a 25% risk of malignancy and warrant cholecystectomy even if asymptomatic.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 32)
  • Q: ___% of bile acids are reabsorbed in the body with the majority of absorption occurring within the ___. A. 95; ileum and colon B. 95; duodenum and ileum C. 80; ileum and colon D. 80; duodenum and ileum (Answer: A — About 80% of conjugated bile acids are reabsorbed in the terminal ileum and, with colonic absorption of secondary bile acids, about 95% of the bile acid pool is reabsorbed via the enterohepatic circulation.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 32)
  • Q: What is the best initial management for an intraoperatively identified minor lateral injury to the common bile duct? A. Placement of a T-tube through the site of injury in the duct B. Primary oversew of the injury intra-operatively C. Resection of the injured portion of the duct with end-to-end anastomosis D. Intraoperative placement of endoscopic biliary stent E. Resection of the bile duct and a roux-en-Y hepatico-jejunostomy reconstruction (Answer: A — Lateral injury to the common bile duct recognized at the time of surgery is best managed with T-tube placement, through the injury itself if it is a small incision in the duct.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 32)
  • Q: In the early postoperative period, what is the most common presentation of a patient with a biliary injury? A. Fever B. Abdominal pain C. Steatorrhea D. Elevated transaminases E. Nausea (Answer: D — In the early postoperative period, patients present either with progressive elevation of liver function tests due to an occluded or stenosed bile duct, or with a bile leak.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 32)
  • Q: After identification of a postoperative biliary stricture, what is the best initial management? A. Operative resection of the involved biliary segment and reconstruction with an end-to-end roux-en-Y hepatico-jejunostomy B. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) with sphincterotomy and stenting of the pancreatic duct C. Transhepatic catheter placement for biliary decompression D. Hepatobiliary iminodiacetic acid (HIDA) scan E. Operative placement of a T-tube at site of biliary stricture (Answer: C — Initial management usually includes transhepatic biliary drainage catheter placement for decompression and for defining the anatomy, location, and extent of the damage.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 32)

Cholangitis

  • Q: Charcot triad is defined as which of the following? A. Fever, pain, jaundice B. Fever, pain, shock C. Fever, altered mental status, jaundice D. Pain, altered mental stasis, jaundice (Answer: A — Charcot triad is fever, epigastric or right upper quadrant pain, and jaundice, present in about two-thirds of patients with cholangitis.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 32)

Acute Pancreatitis

  • Q: According to Ranson's criteria a 67-year-old female patient suspected of acute pancreatitis presenting to the operating room (OR) with sudden onset of severe abdominal pain, a serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST) > 250 U/dL, a WBC > 16,000/mm³, and a blood glucose > 200 mg/dL would receive a disease classification of: A. Severe. B. Mild, uncomplicated. (Answer: A — Per Ranson's prognostic signs (Table 33-7), this patient meets multiple admission criteria (age >55, WBC >16,000/mm³, glucose >200 mg/dL, AST >250 U/dL), classifying the disease as severe.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 33)
  • Q: In the setting of acute pancreatitis, following aggressive fluid resuscitation, what is the most ideal nutritional approach? A. Patient should undergo strict bowel rest until resolution of inflammation. B. Enteral feeding can be started as early as 24 hours following stabilization. C. Parenteral nutrition is preferred, to be started early after stabilization. D. Tube feeding is inferior to parenteral nutrition during inflammation. (Answer: B — Enteral nutrition is now the mainstay of nutritional support; it is no longer acceptable to "rest the pancreas," and parenteral nutrition is more expensive, riskier, and not more effective.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 33)

Chronic Pancreatitis

  • Q: What is the most common cause of chronic pancreatitis? A. Hyperparathyroidism B. Alcohol abuse C. Idiopathic D. Genetic (Answer: C — Idiopathic disease is the most common etiology of chronic pancreatitis (about 42%), with idiopathic and genetic predispositions together comprising more than half of cases.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 33)
  • Q: In patients undergoing endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) for diagnosis and staging of chronic pancreatitis, the population most at risk of developing procedure-induced pancreatitis are those with: A. Calculus disease. B. Intraductal lesions. C. Sphincter of Oddi dysfunction. D. High percentage of parenchymal calcification. (Answer: C — Patients at increased risk of post-ERCP pancreatitis include those with sphincter of Oddi dysfunction and those with a previous history of post-ERCP pancreatitis.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 33)
  • Q: Pain from chronic pancreatitis can be caused by: A. Ductal hypertension. B. Parenchymal disease. C. Obstructive pancreatopathy. D. All of the above. (Answer: D — Pain from chronic pancreatitis has been ascribed to ductal hypertension, parenchymal disease, and obstructive pancreatopathy.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 33)
  • Q: What is the most ideal modality for evaluation of chronic pancreatitis? A. Computed tomography (CT) abdomen pelvis B. Magnetic resonance cholengiopancreatography (MRCP) C. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) D. Endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS) (Answer: D — EUS evaluates subtle changes in 2- to 3-mm structures within the pancreas, may be more sensitive than ERCP for mild disease, and adds cytologic and fluid sampling capability.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 33)
  • Q: Which component of the pancreas is associated with the long-term inflammatory process in chronic pancreatitis, subsequently addressed in surgical resection? A. The head B. The body C. The neck D. The tail (Answer: A — The head of the pancreas is the nidus of the chronic inflammatory process, and removal of the central portion of the head of the gland is the key to long-term resolution of pain.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 33)

Pancreatic Cancer

  • Q: Which of the following have been proven to significantly reduce the rate of pancreatic leak following pancreaticoduodenectomy? A. Stent B. Glue C. Octreotide D. None of the above (Answer: D — Anastomotic modifications, pancreatic duct stents, octreotide, and sealants have all been evaluated without convincing evidence of benefit in preventing pancreatic leak.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 33)
  • Q: Which of the following is the imaging modality of choice for initial diagnosis of exocrine tumors? A. Computed tomography (CT) with oral and intravenous (IV) contrast B. Positron emission tomography-CT (PET-CT) C. CT with four-phase contrast D. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Answer: C — The initial diagnostic imaging test of choice is a multidetector CT scan with four phases of contrast and fine cuts through the pancreas and liver.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 33)
  • Q: A pancreatic cystic neoplasms that is <3 cm has atypical cells present and has a solid component requires: A. A repeat computed tomography (CT) scan in 3 to 6 months. B. A repeat CT scan in 1 year. C. Continued observation. D. Resection. (Answer: D — An enhancing solid component within a cyst is a high-risk stigma of malignancy in the consensus management algorithm (Fig. 33-78), warranting resection.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 33)

Hepatocellular Carcinoma

  • Q: What is the annual conversion rate to hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) for patients with cirrhosis? A. Less than 1% B. 1%–2% C. 2%–6% D. 6%–10% (Answer: C — Cirrhosis is present in 70% to 90% of patients who develop HCC, and the annual conversion rate from cirrhosis is 2% to 6%.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 31)
  • Q: The only FDA-approved systemic chemotherapeutic agent for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is: A. Epirubicin. B. Cisplatin. C. 5-fluorouracil. D. Sorafenib. (Answer: D — The multikinase inhibitor sorafenib has been approved for HCC based on the SHARP trial, which demonstrated a 3-month survival benefit versus placebo.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 31)
  • Q: Based on the standard Milan criteria, which of the following patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) would be eligible for transplantation? A. One 4.5 cm lesion in segment VI with invasion of the right portal vein B. Three lesions confined to the right lobe, with the largest being 2.5 cm C. A single, 5.5 cm lesion in segment II D. Three lesions spread throughout the liver, with the largest being 3.5 cm (Answer: B — The Milan criteria limit eligibility to one tumor <5 cm or up to three tumors <3 cm with no evidence of gross intravascular or extrahepatic spread.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 31)

Liver Abscess and Cysts

  • Q: A 52-year-old man with no travel history, chronic pulmonary obstructive disease, and recurrent diverticulitis presents with increasing right upper quadrant abdominal pain, fevers, and jaundice. Computed tomography (CT) imaging notes a single, 5 cm hypodense lesion with peripheral enhancement in the right lobe of the liver. What is the best treatment for this patient? A. Metronidazole and percutaneous drainage B. Metronidazole C. Mebendazole D. Intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam and percutaneous drainage (Answer: D — Cornerstones of treatment for pyogenic liver abscess are correction of the underlying cause and IV antibiotics covering gram-negative and anaerobic organisms, with percutaneous aspiration/drainage to guide therapy.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 31)
  • Q: Upon return from New Zealand, a 33-year-old woman presents with dull right upper quadrant pain and fevers diagnosed by the presence of echinococcal antigens. What does the computed tomography (CT) imaging demonstrate. A. Multiple, well circumscribed lesions with homogenous arterial enhancement and central scar B. Well-defined low-density round lesions that have enhancement of the wall, somewhat ragged in appearance with a peripheral zone of edema in the left lobe C. Anteroinferior hypodense lesion with a distinct wall and ring-like calcifications in the right lobe D. Asymmetrical nodular peripheral enhancement that is isodense with large vessels and exhibit progressive centripetal enhancement fill-in over time in the right lobe (Answer: C — Hydatid cysts, commonly in the anterior-inferior right lobe, are well-defined hypodense lesions with a distinct wall, and ring-like calcifications of the pericyst are present in 20% to 30% of cases.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 31)
  • Q: The most common benign hepatic lesion is the: A. Hemangioma. B. Simple cyst. C. Adenoma. D. Bile duct hamartoma. (Answer: B — While hemangiomas are the most common solid benign masses, the simple hepatic cyst is the most common benign hepatic lesion overall, with a prevalence of approximately 2.8% to 3.6%.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 31)

Biliary Strictures and Cholangiocarcinoma

  • Q: Which of the following is TRUE in regard to patients with cholangiocarcinoma? A. Surgical resection is the treatment of choice for patients with primary sclerosing cholangitis and cholangiocarcinoma limited to the common bile duct. B. Cholangiocarcinoma is very radiosensitive, therefore external-beam radiation and intravenous 5-fluorouracil constitute destination therapy with 60% 5-year survival. C. 5-year survival rate following the Mayo Clinic protocol and transplantation is 70%. D. Patients with primary sclerosing cholangitis are not candidates for the Mayo Clinic protocol. (Answer: C — The 5-year survival rate for those undergoing transplantation for cholangiocarcinoma at the Mayo Clinic is approximately 70% and compares favorably with resection.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 31)
  • Q: The best initial imaging test for evaluating suspected cholangiocarcinoma includes: A. Percutaneous cholangiography. B. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography. C. Ultrasound. D. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP). E. Hepatobiliary iminodiacetic acid (HIDA) scan. (Answer: C — The initial tests are usually ultrasound or CT; ultrasound can establish the level of obstruction and rule out bile duct stones as the cause of obstructive jaundice.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 32)
  • Q: Patients with a history of choledochal cysts are at increased risk of developing biliary cancer: A. In the gallbladder alone. B. Predominantly intra-hepatic portions of the biliary tree. C. In the distal common bile duct. D. At the site of the previous cyst. E. Throughout the biliary tree. (Answer: E — Patients with choledochal cysts have an increased risk of developing cancer anywhere in the biliary tree, with the incidence highest in the gallbladder.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 32)
  • Q: A 55-year-old man presents with painless jaundice and is found to have a Bismuth-Corlette type II cholangiocarcinoma, which of the following is the next best step? A. Whipple B. Primary resection followed by an hepaticojejunostomy C. Local tumor excision with lymphadenectomy, cholecystectomy, common bile duct (CBD) excision, and bilateral Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomies D. Diagnostic laparoscopy (Answer: B — Perihilar Bismuth-Corlette type I/II tumors without vascular involvement are candidates for tumor excision with portal lymphadenectomy, cholecystectomy, common bile duct excision, and Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy reconstruction.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 32)
  • Q: Which of the following scenarios can result in a 5-year disease survival rate as high as 68%? A. Resectable perihilar cholangiocarcinoma B. Resectable distal cholangiocarcinoma C. Cholangiocarcinoma treated with liver transplantation D. None of the above (Answer: C — Patients who receive liver transplantation for cholangiocarcinoma can experience 5-year disease-free survival rates as high as 68%.) (Source: Schwartz's ABSITE, Ch. 32)
