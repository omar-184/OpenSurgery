Neuroendocrine Tumors and MEN Syndromes

Summary

  • Neuroendocrine tumours (NETs) arise from diffuse neuroendocrine (APUD) cells found throughout the gut, pancreas and elsewhere, and can be sporadic or occur as part of multiple endocrine neoplasia (MEN) syndromes [1][2].
  • Pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours (PNETs) are increasingly diagnosed incidentally; the majority (90%) are nonfunctional, and surgical resection remains the mainstay of treatment for localised disease [3].
  • Small intestinal NETs (SI-NETs, formerly carcinoid tumours) can produce carcinoid syndrome when hepatic or extra-portal metastatic burden overwhelms hepatic serotonin metabolism [4].
  • MEN1, MEN2A, MEN2B (MEN3) and MEN4 are autosomal dominant syndromes combining tumours of the parathyroid, pancreas/duodenum, pituitary, thyroid and adrenal medulla, each requiring coordinated screening and surgical planning [2][5].

Definition

  • Neuroendocrine cells (APUD cells) share amine precursor uptake and decarboxylation capability and a common neuroendocrine differentiation programme; they are found scattered through the GI tract, pancreas (islet cells) and thyroid (C cells), constituting the diffuse neuroendocrine system [1].
  • PNETs are tumours of the pancreatic neuroendocrine cells, classified as functional (producing a hormone excess syndrome) or nonfunctional [3].
  • SI-NETs (carcinoid tumours) arise from enterochromaffin cells at the base of the crypts of Lieberkühn [4].
  • MEN syndromes are rare autosomal dominant familial conditions affecting multiple endocrine organs, with neoplasms developing synchronously or metachronously [2][5].

Pathophysiology

  • MEN1 results from mutation in the MEN1 gene on chromosome 11q13, encoding menin, a histone methyltransferase/cell-cycle regulator and tumour suppressor; it produces the syndrome of the 'three Ps', parathyroid (hyperplasia, nearly 100% penetrance by age 40), pituitary (15–40%, most commonly prolactinoma) and pancreas (PNETs in 30–80% depending on series) [3][5][6].
  • Germline inactivation of one MEN1 allele plus somatic mutation of the other drives development of multiple pancreatic neuroendocrine microtumours; nearly 100% of MEN1 patients develop pancreatic microadenomas (<0.5 cm) by age 50, larger tumours in about 75%, around half develop gastrinomas and 20% insulinomas [3].
  • MEN2A and MEN2B (MEN3) arise from RET proto-oncogene mutations on chromosome 10; MEN2A comprises medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC, ~100%), phaeochromocytoma (10–60%) and hyperparathyroidism (5–20%), while MEN2B/MEN3 comprises MTC, phaeochromocytoma (up to 50%), mucosal/gut neuromas and ganglioneuromatosis, and marfanoid habitus, without hyperparathyroidism [2][5].
  • Familial medullary thyroid carcinoma (FMTC) is a milder RET-associated phenotype with MTC only [2].
  • Von Hippel–Lindau (VHL) disease is autosomal dominant, from failure of VHL protein to degrade hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF), causing PNETs in around 20% of patients (Type I VHL, truncating mutations, 10% PNET risk; Type II, missense mutations, up to 40% PNET risk) plus CNS/retinal haemangioblastomas, renal cysts/carcinoma, phaeochromocytoma and endolymphatic sac tumours [1][3].
  • Tuberous sclerosis (TSC1/TSC2 mutations) and neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) are also associated with PNETs (1% and ~10% respectively), both acting through loss of inhibition of mTOR signalling [3].
  • Sporadic PNETs commonly show mutations in MEN1 (~44%), mTOR-pathway genes (PTEN, TSC1/2, PIK3CA, ~15%) and DAXX/ATRX (~43%); DAXX/ATRX loss or alternative lengthening of telomeres (ALT) positivity confers a worse prognosis [3].
  • Most well-differentiated PNETs (about 90%) highly express somatostatin receptors (SSTR), which is exploited for imaging (DOTA-PET) and therapy (somatostatin analogues, peptide receptor radionuclide therapy) [3].
  • Carcinoid syndrome results when vasoactive amines (serotonin, histamine, kallikrein, bradykinin, prostaglandins) from a NET reach the systemic circulation without first-pass hepatic metabolism by monoamine oxidase, occurring with hepatic metastatic burden exceeding hepatic clearance capacity, or with tumours draining outside the portal system (e.g. bronchial or retroperitoneal primaries) [4].
  • Cardiac (carcinoid heart disease) manifestations occur in 60–70% of advanced disease from tricuspid/pulmonary valve endocardial fibrosis, attributed to high circulating 5-HIAA [4].

Clinical features

  • MEN1 hyperparathyroidism is usually the first manifestation and is typically asymptomatic (urinary symptoms if any); gastrinomas cause the major morbidity of the syndrome (50% multiple, 50% malignant) and prolactinoma is the commonest pituitary tumour [2].
  • MEN2A/2B: MTC is usually the first manifestation, presenting with diarrhoea as the most common symptom (often from bilateral, multifocal disease) and is the number one cause of death in these patients; phaeochromocytoma is often bilateral and nearly always benign in MEN2 (rarely malignant, 3–5%) [1][2].
  • MEN2B additionally features mucosal neuromas and a marfanoid habitus with musculoskeletal abnormalities [2].
  • Insulinoma classically presents with the Whipple triad, hypoglycaemic symptoms (sweating, tremor, blurred vision, confusion, seizures) during fasting or exercise, biochemical hypoglycaemia, and symptomatic relief with restoration of normoglycaemia [3].
  • Gastrinoma (Zollinger-Ellison syndrome) presents with severe reflux, diarrhoea and recurrent ulceration of the stomach, duodenum or upper jejunum [3].
  • Glucagonoma presents with necrolytic migratory erythema, glossitis, cheilitis, hyperglycaemia, weight loss and thromboembolism, with severe protein wasting in symptomatic patients [3].
  • VIPoma (Verner-Morrison/WDHA syndrome) causes profuse watery diarrhoea (6–8 L/day) with hypokalaemia, achlorhydria, hypercalcaemia and flushing, and can cause fatal arrhythmias from electrolyte loss [3].
  • Somatostatinoma causes diabetes, diarrhoea/steatorrhoea and cholelithiasis [3].
  • Pancreatic polypeptidoma (PPoma) produces no clinical syndrome and tends to present late, large, with mass effect [3].
  • Nonfunctional PNETs are often silent until advanced, presenting with mass effect (obstructive jaundice, duodenal obstruction, early satiety) or with liver metastases; more than half have metastasised to nodes or liver at diagnosis [3].
  • SI-NETs most commonly present with abdominal pain, often from intussusception (a polypoid lesion acting as lead point), with symptoms sometimes present for 2–20 years before diagnosis; carcinoid syndrome is the presenting feature in 40% [4].
  • Carcinoid syndrome attacks feature flushing (80% of patients, lasting 5–10 minutes, precipitated by stress, alcohol, large meals or intercourse), watery diarrhoea, abdominal cramps and malabsorption [4].

Etiology

  • MEN1 occurs in approximately 1 per 30,000 individuals, with over 1000 identified MEN1 gene mutations across families [6].
  • Of hereditary PNET syndromes, MEN1 is most common (PNET risk 60–80%), followed by VHL (5–40% depending on mutation type), with NF1 (0–10%), tuberous sclerosis (1%) and Cowden syndrome (case reports only) much rarer [3].
  • Among unselected PNET patients, 6% have a known hereditary syndrome overall, rising to 16% in those diagnosed before age 50 [3].
  • Insulinoma incidence is 1–4 per million/year; gastrinoma 1–1.5 per million/year (20–30% MEN1-associated); glucagonoma about 1 per 10 million; VIPoma 0.05–0.2 per million/year; somatostatinoma 0.01–0.05 per million/year, most functional PNET syndromes are exceedingly rare [3].
  • SI-NETs represent 5–35% of small bowel neoplasms; the appendix is the commonest GI site overall for a primary NET, though 30% of SI-NETs arise in the jejunum/ileum with the most aggressive behaviour [4].
  • Tumour size correlates directly with metastatic risk in SI-NETs: <1 cm carries 20–30% nodal/hepatic spread, 1–2 cm carries 60–80% nodal and 20% hepatic spread, and >2 cm carries >80% nodal and 40–50% hepatic spread [4].

Diagnosis

  • Insulinoma is diagnosed by a supervised 72-hour fast demonstrating hypoglycaemia with inappropriately elevated insulin/proinsulin and C-peptide (excluding surreptitious insulin administration); most are localised by contrast CT or MRI, with EUS or selective arterial calcium stimulation with hepatic venous sampling reserved for small/occult tumours (>94% accuracy for regional localisation) [3].
  • Gastrinoma (Zollinger-Ellison syndrome) is confirmed when fasting gastrin exceeds 10 times the upper limit of normal, or by a secretin stimulation test showing a dramatic gastrin rise at gastric pH <2 (rarely performed given the risk of provoking perforation on PPI withdrawal); most gastrinomas arise in the 'gastrinoma triangle' (cystic duct base, junction of 2nd/3rd duodenum, pancreatic neck), 25% within the pancreas, and are localised with MRI and ⁶⁸Ga-DOTA-PET (lower sensitivity for small duodenal tumours) [3].
  • Glucagonoma is diagnosed by fasting glucagon >500 pg/mL [3].
  • VIPoma is diagnosed by markedly elevated fasting VIP (>200 pg/mL) with profuse diarrhoea [3].
  • PNET imaging relies on biphasic (arterial and venous phase) CT (PNETs classically enhance on the arterial phase, appearing brighter than normal pancreas) or MRI (hypointense T1, enhancing T2), with DOTA-PET (⁶⁸Ga-DOTATATE/DOTATOC, ⁶⁴Cu-DOTATATE) exploiting high SSTR expression to stage the whole body, though it should not be relied on alone given false-positive uncinate/splenic uptake [3].
  • Carcinoid syndrome is confirmed by elevated 24-hour urinary 5-HIAA (the main serotonin metabolite); plasma chromogranin A reflects disease burden [4].
  • CT findings of intussusception from an SI-NET show a distinctive multilayer ringed structure in the ileocolic region [4].
  • MEN1 screening (biochemical, from the second decade in known gene carriers) includes calcium, PTH, gut hormone profile (insulin, glucose, gastrin, IGF-1, chromogranins) and anterior pituitary hormone assay; MEN2 screening uses thyroid ultrasound and calcitonin plus annual urinary/plasma metanephrines and calcium/PTH [5].
  • Genetic testing for MEN1/VHL should be offered as early as possible to first-degree relatives, and considered in patients with suggestive manifestations (especially hypercalcaemia), family history, young age at PNET diagnosis, or multifocal tumours; VHL PNET screening should begin by age 15 [3].

Scoring and Severity

  • WHO grading of NETs (2022 revision) uses the Ki-67 proliferation index: grade 1 (<3% staining), grade 2 (3–20%), and grade 3 (>20%), with grade strongly predicting survival, grade 1 PNETs have 75–90% 5-year survival versus 62–63% for grade 2 and 7–12% for grade 3 [3].
  • Grade 3 tumours are further split into well-differentiated G3 NETs (retain SSTR expression, slower growth, better survival) versus poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas (G3 NECs, absent SSTR2, aggressive, poor survival, Ki-67 often >50–70%) [3].
  • AJCC 8th edition TNM staging for well-differentiated PNETs: T1 <2 cm, T2 2–4 cm, T3 >4 cm (or invading duodenum/bile duct), T4 invades celiac/SMA or adjacent organs; stage I/II have no nodal disease (T1–T3), stage III has nodal disease or T4, stage IV has distant metastases; a multicentre validation cohort found median survival by stage of not-reached, 115, 101 and 72 months respectively [3].
  • Distant metastases are present at diagnosis in about 40% (up to 60% in some series) of PNET patients, with at least 20% having nodal-only disease [3].

Treatment and Management

  • Functional PNETs should be resected regardless of size because of hormonal symptoms and complications; nonfunctional PNETs <1 cm carry low metastatic risk and can be observed, while tumours >2 cm, higher Ki-67, or showing growth should generally be resected (1–2 cm tumours are an area of ongoing controversy/trial evaluation) [3].
  • Parenchymal-sparing resection (enucleation, central pancreatectomy) is preferred in genetic syndromes such as MEN1/VHL where multiple small low-grade tumours recur over a lifetime [3].
  • For metastatic insulinoma, diazoxide improves hypoglycaemia in about 50% of patients (stopped at least a week before surgery for risk of intraoperative hypotension); somatostatin analogues can be less effective (and may worsen hypoglycaemia via glucagon inhibition) given lower SSTR expression in insulinomas [3].
  • Gastrinoma is managed with high-dose PPIs to control acid secretion and reduce ulcer/perforation complications, plus surgical resection, though about half of patients with initially normalised gastrin develop recurrent hypergastrinaemia by 10 years [3].
  • Severely symptomatic glucagonoma may need parenteral nutrition/amino acid infusion before surgery to correct protein wasting and dermatologic symptoms [3].
  • Carcinoid syndrome symptom control uses somatostatin analogues (octreotide, short- and long-acting), effective in 90% of patients, with hepatic-directed therapy (surgical debulking/resection, hepatic artery embolisation, radiofrequency ablation) for hepatic metastatic burden; chemotherapeutic options (doxorubicin, fluorouracil, dacarbazine, interferon-alpha, streptozotocin/fluorouracil combinations, everolimus) have about 20% response rates; radiolabelled somatostatin analogues ('smart bombs', e.g. ¹¹¹In-pentetreotide) are being developed for targeted radiotherapy [4].
  • Five- and 10-year survival for patients with residual abdominal/hepatic NET disease exceeds 60% given the generally slow growth [4].
  • MEN1: correct hyperparathyroidism before other simultaneous endocrine tumours are addressed [2].
  • MEN2A/2B: correct phaeochromocytoma before other simultaneous tumours, particularly before thyroidectomy for MTC [2][5].

Surgeries

  • Enucleation (narrow-margin excision from within the pancreatic parenchyma, at least 3 mm from the main pancreatic duct) is preferred for benign, edge-of-gland lesions such as insulinomas, preserving endocrine/exocrine function at the cost of increased pancreatic fistula risk [3].
  • Distal pancreatectomy (open or laparoscopic, with or without splenectomy) is used for tail/distal body tumours, with an open approach favoured for larger tumours invading/occluding the splenic vein or with nodal/distant metastases requiring cytoreduction [3].
  • Central pancreatectomy is an option for smaller body/neck tumours, better preserving endocrine/exocrine function but with higher pancreatic leak risk (generally managed with a drain) [3].
  • Pancreaticoduodenectomy (Whipple procedure) is required for head/uncinate tumours not amenable to enucleation; laparoscopic/robotic approaches are increasingly used in experienced centres [3].
  • PNETs are less likely than pancreatic adenocarcinoma to invade the SMV and can often be dissected free, with vein resection/reconstruction not precluding resection given the favourable prognosis of resected PNETs [3].
  • Small SI-NETs (<1 cm) can be treated with local excision; all others require segmental bowel and mesenteric resection [4].
  • MEN1-associated hyperparathyroidism requires bilateral cervical exploration with at least subtotal (three-and-a-half gland) parathyroidectomy and cervical thymectomy, or total parathyroidectomy with forearm autotransplantation, given near-universal multigland disease [2][5][6].
  • Most MEN1 pancreatic tumours are benign, so enucleation or distal pancreatectomy is preferred where possible [5].
  • MEN2: planned prophylactic total thyroidectomy is performed based on RET codon-specific risk timing (see Thyroid Cancer page for codon-risk detail); MEN2-associated hyperparathyroidism, usually mild, is treated by excising only the abnormal gland(s) rather than a formal subtotal procedure [5][6].
  • MEN3 (MEN2B) requires therapeutic total thyroidectomy with central compartment lymph node dissection as a minimum, given the impracticality of prophylactic timing in the face of early-onset, aggressive MTC [5].

Complications

  • Reoperative parathyroid surgery in MEN1 carries high rates of persistent/recurrent hyperparathyroidism (up to 62%) and permanent postoperative hypocalcaemia (up to 47%) despite meticulous surgery [6].
  • Pancreatic fistula and leak are recognised complications of PNET surgery, more likely with enucleation and central pancreatectomy, generally managed with surgical or interventional radiology drainage [3].
  • Carcinoid heart disease (tricuspid/pulmonary valve fibrosis) can progress to right heart failure in advanced carcinoid syndrome [4].
  • SI-NET-induced mesenteric desmoplastic reaction can cause vascular sclerosis, bowel kinking, ischaemia and necrosis, occasionally presenting as a surgical emergency [4].
  • Surgical debulking of hepatic NET metastases must be planned to avoid catastrophic injury to the superior mesenteric vessels, which could cause short-gut syndrome [4].

Prognosis

  • Small, localised insulinomas have survival approaching 100% after resection, versus under 30% at 10 years for those >2 cm or with metastases [3].
  • Ten-year survival for resected non-metastatic glucagonoma approaches 100%, versus 52% for resected metastatic glucagonoma [3].
  • VIPoma 5-year survival exceeds 90% for localised disease versus 60% for metastatic disease (21% metastasise to liver) [3].
  • PNET survival correlates strongly with grade (75–90% at grade 1, 62–63% at grade 2, 7–12% at grade 3, all at 5 years) and AJCC stage (median survival not reached in stage I down to 72 months in stage IV) [3].
  • SI-NET/carcinoid patients with residual abdominal or hepatic tumour burden managed with debulking/resection and adjunctive therapy have 5- and 10-year survival exceeding 60% given typically slow tumour growth [4].
  • Metastatic PNETs are among the most common causes of disease-specific death in MEN1, alongside thymic neuroendocrine neoplasms [3].
  • MTC (the dominant cause of death in MEN2/3) prognosis and thyroid-specific detail is covered on the Thyroid Cancer page [2].
