Adrenal Tumors

Summary

  • Adrenal tumours range from incidentally discovered non-functioning adenomas to hormonally active tumours (Cushing's syndrome, Conn's syndrome/primary hyperaldosteronism, phaeochromocytoma) and rare adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC) [1][2].
  • The key questions in any adrenal mass are whether it is functioning, whether it is malignant, and whether resection is indicated [1].
  • Diagnosis follows a stepwise biochemical-then-radiological approach, and laparoscopic adrenalectomy is now standard for most benign and functioning tumours, with open surgery reserved for large or invasive disease [1][2].

Definition

  • An adrenal incidentaloma is an asymptomatic adrenal mass discovered by chance on imaging performed for a non-adrenal indication [1][2].
  • Cushing's syndrome refers to any cause of hypercortisolism, whether from a pituitary ACTH-secreting adenoma (Cushing's disease), ectopic ACTH secretion, exogenous steroid administration, or autonomous adrenal cortisol secretion (adrenal/ACTH-independent Cushing's syndrome) [1][3].
  • Conn's syndrome (primary hyperaldosteronism/primary aldosteronism) is a heterogeneous group of disorders characterised by hypertension and inappropriately raised plasma aldosterone [1].
  • Phaeochromocytomas (PCCs) arise from neuroectodermal tissue of the adrenal medulla; tumours from extra-adrenal sympathetic/parasympathetic ganglia are paragangliomas (PGLs); together these are termed PPGL [1].
  • Adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC) is a rare, aggressive malignancy arising from the adrenal cortex [1].

Pathophysiology

  • The adrenal cortex (90% of the gland) is zonally arranged: the outer zona glomerulosa secretes aldosterone, the (largest, 70%) zona fasciculata secretes cortisol, and the inner zona reticularis secretes androgens [1][4].
  • Aldosterone secretion is regulated chiefly by the renin–angiotensin system and serum potassium; cortisol secretion is regulated by ACTH under hypothalamic CRH control with negative feedback [1].
  • The adrenal medulla, derived from neural-crest ectodermal chromaffin cells, synthesises catecholamines, predominantly adrenaline (80%) via the enzyme PNMT, which is exclusive to the adrenal medulla (extra-adrenal paraganglia cannot produce adrenaline) [1][4].
  • Primary hyperaldosteronism is caused by an aldosterone-producing adenoma (APA, 30–40% of cases; "canary tumour," typically 10–20 mm, often KCNJ5-mutated), bilateral (idiopathic) adrenal hyperplasia, unilateral hyperplasia, ACC, or rare familial hyperaldosteronism (FH types I–IV, autosomal dominant) [1].
  • Cushing's syndrome due to adrenal pathology is most often an adrenocortical adenoma, less commonly bilateral macronodular adrenal hyperplasia (BMAH) or ACC; ACTH-secreting pituitary tumours cause 70–80% of all Cushing's syndrome and ectopic ACTH (e.g. small-cell lung cancer) causes about 10% [1].
  • PPGL is sporadic in about 70% of cases; the remainder occur within hereditary syndromes, MEN2, von Hippel–Lindau (VHL) disease, neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1), and hereditary PPGL syndromes from SDH subunit, MAX or TMEM127 mutations [1].
  • VHL patients develop PCC (overproducing only noradrenaline) more often than PGL; NF1 patients develop PCC in about 7% (96% of their PPGLs being PCC) [1].
  • ACC arises sporadically in most cases but occurs as part of MEN1, familial adenomatous polyposis, Li–Fraumeni and Lynch syndromes in a minority [1].
  • Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (21-hydroxylase deficiency in 90–95% of cases) causes excess ACTH-driven androgenic cortisol precursors, producing virilisation and, in the salt-wasting form, hypotension from aldosterone deficiency [1][4].

Clinical features

  • Adrenal incidentalomas are by definition asymptomatic at presentation, found in about 5% of high-resolution abdominal scans (1–2% per the ABSITE Review, rising with age); careful history/examination may reveal features of a functional lesion [2][4].
  • Cushing's syndrome produces a multisystem picture: obesity/central adiposity with buffalo hump and moon face (90%), hypertension (85%), facial plethora (70%), hirsutism (75%), glucose intolerance/diabetes (75%), hyperlipidaemia (70%), abdominal striae (50%), acne (35%), easy bruising (35%), osteoporosis (80%), proximal myopathy (65%), mood disturbance (85%), menstrual disorders (70%), and renal stones (50%) [1].
  • Conn's syndrome/primary hyperaldosteronism presents chiefly with hypertension, sometimes refractory, and (where hypokalaemic) muscle weakness, cramps, fatigue, polyuria/polydipsia; about half of patients are normokalaemic [2][4].
  • Phaeochromocytoma classically produces paroxysmal or continuous hypertension (80–90%), headache (60–90%), sweating (50–70%), palpitations (50–70%), pallor, weight loss, hyperglycaemia and psychological effects, with 90% of patients having the triad of headache, palpitations and sweating in the presence of an adrenal tumour [1].
  • Paroxysms can be triggered by exercise, anaesthesia induction, contrast media, tricyclics, metoclopramide and opiates [1].
  • ACC presents with hormone excess (50–60%, typically Cushing's or a mixed Cushing's/virilisation picture in women), abdominal/back pain from mass effect (30–40%), or as an incidentaloma (about one in six) [1].
  • Children with ACC display virilisation 90% of the time; feminisation in men or masculinisation in women should raise suspicion of malignancy [2][4].

Etiology

  • The risk of malignancy in an unselected adrenal incidentaloma is low (about 0.1–1%), and tumours under 4 cm are rarely malignant (≤1%) [2].
  • Common metastases to the adrenal gland arise from lung cancer (most common), breast cancer, melanoma and renal cell carcinoma; a cancer history with a new adrenal mass warrants biopsy [1][4].
  • Primary hyperaldosteronism is the most common cause of endocrine hypertension, found in up to one-fifth of patients investigated for hypertension, twice as common in women, and more prevalent with drug-resistant hypertension [1].
  • Adrenal Cushing's syndrome is uncommon (1–2 per million per year) relative to pituitary-dependent disease (6–7 per million per year); iatrogenic Cushing's from exogenous corticosteroids is likely the most prevalent overall cause [1][4].
  • PCC incidence is about 0.6 per 100,000 (ABSITE Review cites a "10% rule" (malignant, bilateral, in children, familial, extra-adrenal) each occurring in roughly 10% of cases), with a right-sided predominance and extra-adrenal tumours more likely to be malignant [1][4].
  • Pheochromocytoma is rarely malignant in MEN2 (3–5%) but often malignant with SDHB mutations (up to 50%) [1].
  • ACC has a bimodal age distribution (under 5 years and the fourth–fifth decades) with a female predominance and is very aggressive, with 80% having advanced disease at diagnosis [1][4].

Diagnosis

  • Any adrenal mass work-up should confirm the diagnosis biochemically, render the patient safe (treat hypertension, hypoglycaemia, hypokalaemia), decide if localisation studies are needed, and then decide on surgery and its approach [1].
  • For an incidentaloma, functionality is assessed with at minimum a 1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test (Cushing's), 24-hour urinary/plasma metanephrines (phaeochromocytoma), and (in hypertensive patients) plasma potassium and aldosterone:renin ratio (Conn's) [1][2].
  • Malignancy is best assessed with non-contrast CT and Hounsfield unit (HU) density: benign lesions are low-density (≤10 HU); suspicious features include diameter >40 mm, contrast washout <40% relative/<60% absolute, no signal drop on MRI chemical-shift out-of-phase imaging, and FDG-PET positivity [1].
  • Radiological suspicion of ACC includes size >6 cm, heterogeneous appearance, necrosis, local invasion or metastasis (typically lung/liver) [1].
  • Adrenal biopsy is rarely indicated (useful mainly for metastases), cannot reliably distinguish benign from malignant primary adrenal tumours, and risks tumour seeding in ACC or a hypertensive crisis if performed on an unsuspected phaeochromocytoma [1][2].
  • Primary hyperaldosteronism diagnosis requires a non-suppressed plasma aldosterone with suppressed plasma renin activity; an aldosterone:renin ratio (ARR) >850 (or >2000 by another cited threshold) is suggestive, and interfering antihypertensives (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, direct renin inhibitors, aldosterone antagonists) should be stopped for two weeks beforehand [1][2].
  • High-resolution adrenal CT is first-line, but adrenal vein sampling (AVS) is the gold standard for lateralising unilateral disease, particularly over age 35 or with negative/bilateral imaging; 11C-metomidate PET is a useful adjunct with 86% specificity and 76% sensitivity [1].
  • Cushing's syndrome diagnosis (Endocrine Society 2008 criteria) requires two abnormal tests among late-night salivary cortisol, 24-hour urinary free cortisol, and overnight 1 mg dexamethasone suppression test; serum ACTH then differentiates ACTH-dependent (raised, pituitary MRI ± inferior petrosal sinus sampling) from ACTH-independent (suppressed, adrenal CT/MRI) disease [1].
  • A raised ACTH with failure to suppress on high-dose dexamethasone suppression testing suggests ectopic ACTH (e.g. small-cell lung cancer) rather than pituitary disease [4].
  • Phaeochromocytoma/paraganglioma is diagnosed by elevated plasma and/or 24-hour urinary fractionated metanephrines (sensitivity 99%/97%, superior to catecholamine measurement at 86%/84%); a result over four times the upper limit of normal is 100% diagnostic [1].
  • Imaging is by CT or MRI; if negative or extra-adrenal disease is suspected, ¹²³I-MIBG (80–90% sensitive) or ¹¹¹In-octreotide scanning (50–70% sensitive) is used, with the MIBG scan considered best for localising an occult tumour [1][4].
  • Venography/biopsy should be avoided in suspected phaeochromocytoma owing to the risk of precipitating a hypertensive crisis; intravenous contrast should also be used cautiously for the same reason [3][4].
  • Octreotide/somatostatin-receptor scans are not useful for phaeochromocytoma as it does not overexpress somatostatin receptors [3].
  • ACC biochemical work-up screens for glucocorticoid excess, adrenal androgens/oestradiol, aldosterone:renin ratio if hypertensive, and metanephrines in all patients to exclude phaeochromocytoma [1].

Scoring and Severity

  • The Weiss histopathological scoring system distinguishes benign from malignant adrenocortical tumours when local invasion/metastasis are absent, using five criteria (>6 mitoses/50 high-power fields, ≤25% clear cytoplasm cells, abnormal mitoses, necrosis, capsular invasion), scoring 2 points each for the first two criteria present and 1 point each for the remaining three; a total score ≥3 suggests malignant behaviour, and Ki-67 proliferation index is increasingly used alongside it [1][2].
  • The ENSAT staging system for ACC reports 5-year disease-specific survival of 82% (stage I, T1N0M0, tumour <5 cm), 61% (stage II, T2N0M0, >5 cm), 50% (stage III, local infiltration/vena cava invasion/positive nodes without distant metastases) and 13% (stage IV, distant metastases) [1].
  • For pheochromocytoma, the PASS (Pheochromocytoma of the Adrenal gland Scaled Score) and Ki-67 index help distinguish malignant from benign tumours, alongside vascular invasion or capsular breach [1].
  • Hereditary PPGL syndromes carry gene-specific malignancy risk (e.g.
  • SDHB 34–97%, MAX 25%, SDHD <5%) [1].

Treatment and Management

  • Small (<40 mm), benign, non-functioning incidentalomas do not require surgery but warrant follow-up imaging at 6 months, since tumours >30 mm carry increased risk of developing hyperfunction over time [1].
  • Adrenalectomy is recommended for all tumours >40 mm, those with malignant imaging features, those showing significant growth, and all functioning tumours causing hormone excess [1][2].
  • Conn's syndrome: hypertension/hypokalaemia should be corrected preoperatively; spironolactone (or eplerenone) is used medically, with response to a trial of aldosterone antagonist predicting surgical outcome; unilateral APA is treated by minimally invasive adrenalectomy, while bilateral disease is generally managed medically with aldosterone antagonists unless a dominant nodule is confirmed by lateralisation [1][2].
  • About 50% of Conn's patients stop all antihypertensives post-operatively, and a further 40% reduce their requirement [2].
  • Cushing's syndrome: metyrapone, ketoconazole, or IV etomidate (in critically ill patients) reduce cortisol synthesis preoperatively or as primary therapy when surgery is not possible [1][4].
  • ACTH-secreting pituitary tumours are treated by trans-sphenoidal resection ± radiotherapy; unilateral adrenal adenoma is treated by adrenalectomy; bilateral ACTH-independent disease may need bilateral adrenalectomy in severe/symmetric disease, or excision of the larger gland in asymmetric disease [1][4].
  • Perioperative hydrocortisone (50–100 mg IV) is required for any adrenalectomy for cortisol excess, with post-operative hydrocortisone replacement (15–25 mg/day) tapered as the contralateral gland recovers (which may take up to a year), confirmed with a Synacthen test [1][2].
  • Phaeochromocytoma requires preoperative alpha-blockade (phenoxybenzamine, titrated from 20 mg to 100–160 mg/day until postural hypotension develops, or alternatives such as doxazosin/prazosin), with beta-blockade added only after adequate alpha-blockade to avoid unopposed alpha-mediated hypertensive crisis; this reduced perioperative mortality from 20–45% to under 3% [1][4].
  • Volume repletion is also part of preoperative preparation [3][4].
  • Metyrosine (inhibits tyrosine hydroxylase) can be used preoperatively or for unresectable disease [4].
  • For ACC, first-line management of tumours <6 cm without metastasis may be laparoscopic (converting to open if invasion is found); tumours >6 cm require open radical en bloc resection; disseminated disease is managed with palliative mitotane ± chemotherapy, with surgery reconsidered if there is significant regression at 3–6 months [1].
  • Adjuvant mitotane is recommended for high-risk ACC (size >5 cm, Ki-67 >10%, intraoperative rupture, tumour thrombus) and metastatic disease, for up to 5 years, with etoposide-doxorubicin-cisplatin (EDP) chemotherapy as an option on progression [1][4].
  • Isolated adrenal metastases (from renal, lung or colorectal primaries) may be resected if disease is otherwise controlled and isolated on staging [1].

Surgeries

  • Laparoscopic adrenalectomy (transperitoneal or posterior retroperitoneal) is the procedure of choice for the great majority of adrenal incidentalomas, benign functioning tumours, and phaeochromocytomas up to and including large (>10 cm) tumours, and for suspicious lesions <60 mm without local invasion [1][2].
  • Open adrenalectomy is mandated for tumours with radiological signs of malignancy, local invasion, tumours >6 cm suspicious for ACC, and for extra-adrenal paraganglioma given the technical challenge of the retroperitoneal great-vessel relationship and higher recurrence risk with hereditary disease [1][2].
  • During phaeochromocytoma surgery, the adrenal vein should be ligated early to limit catecholamine spillage from tumour manipulation, with invasive arterial and central venous monitoring and vasopressor/vasodilator agents (e.g. nitroprusside, phenylephrine) and antiarrhythmics available [1][4].
  • Malignant phaeochromocytoma with direct invasion is treated by laparotomy and en bloc excision of involved organs; excision of the primary is still recommended even with metastases, to improve symptom control and the efficacy of adjuvant MIBG/octreotide therapy [1].
  • ACC surgery aims for R0 resection; radical adrenalectomy (including the kidney) is advised, with debulking helping symptom control and prolonging survival even when cure is not possible [1][4].
  • Advantages of laparoscopic over open adrenalectomy include decreased blood loss, faster return to work, and decreased narcotic use, at the cost of longer operative time [3].

Complications

  • Postoperative care after unilateral adrenalectomy for Cushing's requires hydrocortisone supplementation because the contralateral gland remains suppressed, potentially for up to a year [1].
  • Bilateral adrenalectomy for Cushing's disease after failed pituitary surgery can lead to Nelson's syndrome (continued ACTH/POMC secretion from the pituitary adenoma causing hyperpigmentation and diminished visual fields) in about 10% of patients [1][3].
  • Cushing's syndrome itself predisposes to venous thromboembolism, cardiac events, infection and poor wound healing, warranting thromboprophylaxis and perioperative antibiotics [1].
  • Postoperative complications after phaeochromocytoma surgery include persistent hypertension, hypotension, hypoglycaemia, bronchospasm, arrhythmias, intracerebral haemorrhage, cardiac failure and myocardial infarction; patients should be observed for 24 hours given the risk of hypovolaemia and hypoglycaemia [1][4].
  • Steroids are not required to prevent adrenal insufficiency after phaeochromocytoma surgery (unlike Cushing's surgery) [3].
  • Contralateral adrenalectomy after previous nephrectomy/adrenalectomy for a metastasis will render the patient steroid-dependent [1].
  • Adrenal haemorrhage is a rare but serious complication (from sepsis, myocardial infarction, anticoagulation, trauma, surgery or antiphospholipid syndrome) that can cause adrenal insufficiency and hypovolaemic shock, usually managed conservatively, occasionally with interventional radiology [1].

Prognosis

  • Overall survival for malignant phaeochromocytoma is under 50% at 5 years, with a highly variable natural history [1].
  • ACC carries a poor prognosis overall, reflecting its tendency to present late; the ABSITE Review reports an overall 5-year survival of 20%, while ENSAT-staged 5-year disease-specific survival ranges from 82% (stage I) down to 13% (stage IV) [1][4].
  • Untreated Cushing's syndrome carries a fivefold increased risk of cardiovascular death, and mortality approaches 50% at 5 years if untreated per the Oxford Handbook [1][2].
  • Adrenal incidentaloma malignancy risk correlates with size: overall risk is about 0.1%, rising to roughly 10% for tumours over 4 cm [2].
